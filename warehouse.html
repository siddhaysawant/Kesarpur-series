A story about the commodity market, in sixteen chapters

The Kesarpur Warehouse

At harvest, Gopal's farmer group can sell its mustard seed for ₹5,200 a quintal. Seven months later, the same seed sells for ₹6,100. Between those two prices sit a warehouse, a bank loan, an exchange and a whole market. Follow the mustard, the gold in Rukhsana's cupboard and the diesel in Harjeet's tanks, and you'll understand how the world trades the things it grows, digs up and burns.

Part one

From field to market

Prologue

Too much mustard in March

Gopal, the mango farmer from the derivatives story, now leads a farmer producer organisation of 300 families. In the cool months after the monsoon, many of them grow mustard, whose seed is crushed into the oil that every Kesarpur kitchen cooks with, and that Sunrise Foods uses for its pickles.

In March, the whole district harvests at once. Trucks queue outside the mandi. Buyers know the farmers need cash for loans, school fees and the next crop, and the price drops to ₹5,200 a quintal (100 kg). By October, the seed has been bought up, crushed and eaten, and mills are hunting for more. The price has climbed to ₹6,100.

The farmers grew the seed. Someone else earned the extra ₹900 a quintal for simply holding it for seven months. Gopal wants to know why it can't be them.

To answer him, we have to understand how raw goods are priced, stored and traded: the commodity market.

Seventh storyThis follows the bond, mutual fund, stock, derivatives, money market and forex stories. Gopal, Lakshmi, Iyer, Rukhsana, Kamala and Rohit all return.
QuintalThe standard unit for crops in Indian mandis: 100 kilograms. Gold is quoted per 10 grams, crude oil per barrel (159 litres).
Chapter 1

What a commodity is

A commodity is a basic good where one unit of a given grade is as good as another. A quintal of grade-A mustard seed from Gopal's field is interchangeable with a quintal from the next village. A gram of 24-carat gold in Kesarpur is the same as a gram in Zurich. That sameness, called being fungible, is what lets commodities be traded in bulk, stored together and priced on exchanges.

A Sunrise pickle jar, by contrast, is a branded product. Customers pay for Lakshmi's recipe, not just mangoes and oil.

Commodities are different from shares and bonds in an important way: they pay nothing. A share pays dividends; a bond pays interest. A sack of mustard or a gold bar just sits there, and storing it costs money. The only way to profit from holding a commodity is for its price to rise. That fact shapes almost everything in this story.

Hard and softHard commodities are mined or extracted: metals, oil. Soft commodities are grown: crops, livestock.
Grade matters"Mustard" isn't one thing. Exchanges define exact grades: oil content, moisture, impurities. Prices adjust for quality differences.
Chapter 2

The mandi

Most Indian crops are first sold in a mandi, a regulated market yard. Farmers bring their produce, commission agents arrange auctions, and traders and mills bid. This is the spot market: goods change hands now, for cash, at today's price. Here's how mustard travels from Gopal's field to Rukhsana's kitchen, and how the price grows along the way.

Minimum support price

For about two dozen crops, the government announces a minimum support price (MSP) each season. For crops like wheat and rice, government agencies buy large amounts at that price. For many others, including mustard, government buying is more limited, so market prices can still fall below MSP at harvest.

e-NAM

Since 2016, the national electronic agriculture market lets traders bid online for produce in participating mandis across states, widening the pool of buyers for a farmer's lot.

Farmer groups

Farmer producer organisations (FPOs) like Gopal's pool their members' crops. A large, graded lot attracts better buyers, can be stored, and can even be sold on an exchange.

Chapter 3

The seasons of price

Iyer at the cooperative bank has watched mustard prices for years. The pattern repeats: low at harvest, when supply floods in; higher through the lean months, as stocks are used up; then down again when the next crop's size becomes clear.

Some crops swing far harder. Onions and tomatoes can't be stored long, so one bad monsoon or a heatwave can multiply prices within weeks, and a bumper crop can push them so low that farmers dump produce on the road. That's why kitchen staples sometimes make headlines, and why governments step in with export bans, stock limits and imports.

Prices also depend on the whole world. India imports a large share of its edible oils, such as palm and soybean oil. If palm oil from Indonesia and Malaysia gets cheaper, mills buy less mustard, and Gopal's price falls even with an average Indian crop.

Supply shocksCommodity supply can't respond quickly. A farmer can't grow more mustard until next season; a mine takes years to open. So small shortages cause big price jumps.
Rabi and kharifIndia's two main crop seasons: kharif, sown with the monsoon (rice, cotton, soybean), and rabi, sown in winter and harvested in spring (wheat, mustard, chana).
Chapter 4

The warehouse and its receipt

Gopal's answer is the warehouse on the Kesarpur highway. It's registered with India's warehousing regulator (WDRA). Here's what happens when the FPO brings 500 quintals of mustard in March:

  • The seed is tested, graded, weighed, fumigated and stacked. The warehouse is insured.
  • The FPO receives an electronic negotiable warehouse receipt (e-NWR): a digital document saying exactly what's stored, how much and of what grade. It can be transferred to a buyer without moving a single sack.
  • Iyer's bank lends against the receipt: about 70% of the seed's value, at 9% a year. The farmers get cash now to repay their crop loans, without selling at the harvest low.

But storage isn't free, and the price might not rise. Try the decision yourself.

For 500 quintals₹2,87,000

Storage is a bet that the price will rise by more than the cost of waiting. It usually does for mustard, but not always. In a year with a huge next crop or cheap imported oil, the October price can be lower than March's, and the farmers lose twice: a lower price and the storage costs. Chapter 5 shows how they can protect against that.

Cost of carryStorage, insurance and interest: the cost of holding a commodity over time. In the derivatives story, the same idea set futures prices.
Pledge financeLoans against warehouse receipts let farmers avoid distress sales. The bank's security is the stored grain itself.
Warehouse riskReceipts are only as good as the warehouse. Fraud, missing stock or poor storage have hit lenders and traders before, which is why registration, audits and insurance matter.
Chapter 5

The commodity exchange

Gopal's group can protect its stored mustard by selling futures, exactly like the mango contract story, but on a commodity exchange. India's two main commodity exchanges were set up in 2003: NCDEX, focused on farm goods, and MCX, focused on metals and energy. Since 2015, they've been regulated by SEBI.

A futures contract on an exchange specifies everything: the commodity, grade, quantity, delivery centre and month. If Gopal's FPO sells October futures at ₹6,000 in March, it has locked in roughly that price. If the October spot price falls to ₹5,000, the gain on the futures roughly offsets the lower price for the seed. If it rises to ₹6,800, the futures lose, but the seed sells for more.

Some contracts end in delivery: the seller places goods in an exchange-approved warehouse, and the buyer receives the warehouse receipt. That link to physical goods keeps futures prices honest. Most traders, though, close their positions before expiry.

There's a catch for farm goods in India. Since late 2021, SEBI has suspended new futures trading in several sensitive farm commodities, including wheat, chana, mustard and soybean, citing concerns about food inflation, and has extended the suspension several times. So in practice Gopal's group might use forward deals with mills, or futures in other crops still trading. The debate continues: futures help farmers find prices and hedge, but governments worry that speculation can push up food prices.

Price discoveryFutures prices tell everyone what the market expects for future months. Mills, traders and farmers use them even if they never trade.
Options tooMCX and NCDEX also list options on some commodity futures, the same "insurance" idea as Gopal's mango put.
Chapter 6

Contango and backwardation

Look at the futures prices for several delivery months side by side, and you get a futures curve. Its shape tells a story about supply.

Normally, later months cost more, to pay for storage, insurance and interest. This upward slope is contango. But when there's a shortage right now, buyers will pay more for goods today than for goods later. The curve slopes down. This is backwardation. The extra value of having the goods in hand, rather than on paper, is called the convenience yield.

The shape matters to investors who never touch a sack. A fund that holds commodity futures must keep "rolling" them: selling the expiring month and buying the next. In contango, it keeps selling cheap and buying dear, which drains returns even if the spot price doesn't move. That's one reason commodity funds can lag the commodity's price over time.

Roll yieldThe gain or loss from replacing expiring futures with later ones. Negative in contango, positive in backwardation.

Gold

Chapter 7

Gold in Rukhsana's cupboard

Rukhsana, Salim's mother from the forex story, is buying gold for her daughter's wedding. In her steel cupboard is a small cloth bundle: her mother's bangles, her own wedding necklace, coins bought one at a time over thirty years. For many Indian families, gold is savings, insurance and heirloom in one. India is one of the world's two largest buyers of gold, alongside China, and imports almost all of it.

At the jeweller, she learns the vocabulary of gold:

Since 2021, gold jewellery sold in most of India must be hallmarked by the Bureau of Indian Standards. Each piece carries a six-digit code (HUID) that can be checked in the government's BIS Care app, showing its purity.

Then there's the bill. Let's price a 20-gram, 22K necklace.

Chapter 8

How gold is priced in India

Rukhsana asks why the gold rate changes every morning. The answer connects three markets: global gold, forex and government taxes.

Gold is priced worldwide in US dollars per troy ounce (31.1035 grams), mainly in London and New York. To get the Indian price, you convert ounces to grams, dollars to rupees, then add import duty and GST.

Indian price per 10 g, 24K₹73,715
Before duty and GST₹67,517
₹ per 10 g ≈ $ per ounce × ₹ per $ ÷ 31.1035 × 10 × (1 + duty) × (1 + 3% GST)

That's why gold in India can rise even when global gold is flat: if the rupee falls from 84 to 88 to the dollar, Indian gold gets about 5% dearer. Try it on the slider. Import duty matters too: India cut gold import duty sharply in 2024, and local prices fell on the day of the announcement. Local premiums or discounts, set by supply and demand at Indian dealers, add a little more on top.

Troy ounceThe traditional unit for precious metals: 31.1035 g, a little heavier than an ordinary ounce (28.35 g).
Link to forexA weaker rupee makes every imported commodity dearer in India: gold, oil, edible oil, fertiliser. That's why the forex and commodity markets move together.
Chapter 9

Gold without the cupboard

Rohit, Kamala's grandson, wants some gold in his savings as protection, but he doesn't want jewellery or a locker. He compares the options.

Rohit chooses a gold ETF through his existing demat account, adding a little each month. He also checks the latest tax rules, because gold ETFs, gold funds and physical gold have been taxed differently and the rules changed in 2024.

Gold ETFAn exchange-traded fund where each unit represents a small, fixed quantity of physical gold held in a vault. Silver ETFs have been allowed in India since 2022.
Regulation mattersAn ETF or fund is regulated by SEBI and audited. A private app's "digital gold" sits outside that regulation.
Chapter 10

Why gold moves

Gold pays no interest and has limited industrial use. So why does its price swing, and why has it risen so much over two decades?

Real interest rates

Holding gold means giving up interest. When interest rates after inflation are high, gold is less attractive; when they're low or negative, gold shines. The gold fall of 2013–2015 came as US rates were expected to rise.

Fear

In wars, financial crises and pandemics, investors buy gold as a store of value that no government can print. The 2008 crisis and 2020 pandemic both pushed gold up.

The dollar

Gold is priced in dollars, so a weaker dollar usually means a higher gold price, and vice versa.

Central banks

Central banks, including the RBI, hold gold in their reserves. In recent years they've bought record amounts, over 1,000 tonnes a year globally in 2022–2024, partly to depend less on the dollar.

Indian and Chinese demand

Weddings, festivals like Akshaya Tritiya and Dhanteras, and good harvests lift jewellery demand. But very high prices can cut buying.

What gold doesn't do

Gold can go nowhere for years. From 2011 to 2019, its dollar price was roughly flat or lower. It protects against some crises, not against everything.

Safe havenAn asset people rush to in a crisis. Gold, the US dollar and US government bonds are the classic ones.
Part three

Energy, metals and the economy

Chapter 11

Harjeet's diesel

Harjeet runs the petrol pump on the Kesarpur highway. Every truck carrying Gopal's mustard and every bus to the district hospital fills up there. He knows the world's oil news before most people, because his customers complain within days.

India imports roughly 85% of the crude oil it uses. Crude is priced in dollars per barrel, usually benchmarked to Brent. The price swings with how much OPEC+ countries pump, the strength of world demand, wars and sanctions, US shale output, and the season.

But crude is only part of what Harjeet's customers pay. Here's how an illustrative litre of petrol is built.

Taxes make up a large share, so pump prices don't move one-for-one with crude. When oil spiked to about $147 a barrel in July 2008, or when it collapsed in 2020, governments and oil companies adjusted taxes and margins, cushioning the change.

Oil has had some of the wildest moves of any market. In April 2020, with the world in lockdown and storage tanks full, a US crude futures contract (WTI) briefly traded below zero: sellers paid buyers to take oil they had no place to put. It's the clearest lesson in this story that a commodity isn't just a price on a screen; it's a physical thing that has to go somewhere.

Brent and WTIThe two main benchmark crude prices: Brent (North Sea) is used for most oil India buys; WTI is the US benchmark.
Inflation linkDiesel moves vegetables, grain and goods across India. A sharp oil rise feeds into the cost of almost everything.
Chapter 12

Copper, steel and the economy

When Sunrise built its new factory, the price of steel, copper wiring and aluminium sheets mattered as much as mangoes. Base metals are the raw materials of buildings, cars, power lines and phones, so their prices rise when the world is building and fall when it slows. Copper is sometimes nicknamed "Dr. Copper" because its price is seen as a quick reading of the world economy's health.

For two decades, China's construction boom drove metal demand. In the 2000s, rising Chinese demand pushed up copper, iron ore and oil together, a long rise called a commodity super cycle. When Chinese growth slowed in the 2010s, many metal prices fell sharply.

Today, a new source of demand is the energy transition: electric cars, solar panels, wind turbines and power grids need large amounts of copper, lithium, nickel and other metals. Mines take a decade or more to develop, so prices can stay high while supply catches up.

In India, MCX lists futures on copper, aluminium, zinc, lead and nickel. Globally, the main metals exchange is the London Metal Exchange.

CyclicalPrices that rise and fall with the economic cycle. Base metals are among the most cyclical things you can buy.
Commodity-linked sharesShares of mining, steel and oil companies often move with commodity prices, magnified by their own debt and costs.
Chapter 13

Hoarders, bubbles and bans

Commodity markets attract speculation, and sometimes manipulation, because supplies are limited and everyone needs the goods.

Kamala's silverAfter her options losses, Kamala tried silver futures on MCX, persuaded that "silver must catch up with gold". She bought with margin. A sudden 8% fall in two days wiped out most of her deposit. The leverage lessons from the derivatives story apply in full.
The Hunt brothers, 1980Two US oil billionaires tried to corner the world's silver market, buying huge amounts of physical silver and futures. The price rose from about $6 an ounce in early 1979 to nearly $50 in January 1980. Exchanges changed the rules to limit buying on borrowed money, and the price collapsed on "Silver Thursday" in March 1980.
NSEL, 2013On India's National Spot Exchange, some contracts were sold as safe commodity trades. The exchange suspended trading in 2013, and payments of about ₹5,600 crore to investors defaulted, after it emerged that much of the stock supposedly backing them did not exist in the warehouses.
HoardingTraders who hold back essential goods to push up prices can be prosecuted. Under the Essential Commodities Act, governments can impose stock limits on items like pulses, onions and edible oils when prices spike.
Export bans and dutiesTo keep food affordable at home, India has at times banned or restricted exports of wheat, rice, onions and sugar. That protects consumers but can cut farmers' prices and shake world markets.
CornerBuying up so much of a commodity that others who need it must pay whatever you ask. Illegal in regulated markets, and usually ends badly for the attempter.
Chapter 14

The commodity market at scale

Zoom out from Kesarpur. Commodity markets connect farmers in Punjab, oil fields in the Gulf, copper mines in Chile and gold vaults in London. They have three layers: the physical market (mandis, ports, warehouses, refineries), the derivatives market (futures and options on exchanges), and the investment market (ETFs, funds and shares linked to commodities).

Exchanges

India: MCX (metals and energy), NCDEX (farm goods), and commodity segments on NSE and BSE. Abroad: CME Group (including NYMEX and COMEX) in the US, ICE (Brent crude) and the London Metal Exchange.

Hedgers

Farmers and FPOs, food processors like Sunrise, oil refiners, airlines, jewellers, metal fabricators: people with real exposure to prices.

Speculators and arbitrageurs

Traders, funds and individuals betting on prices, and arbitrageurs keeping spot, futures and international prices in line.

Big physical players

Global trading houses buy, store, ship and blend commodities around the world, earning from price differences between places and times.

Governments

MSP and public procurement, buffer stocks held by the Food Corporation of India, strategic petroleum reserves, import duties, export bans and stock limits. Governments are bigger players in commodities than in almost any other market.

Regulators

SEBI regulates commodity derivatives; the warehousing regulator (WDRA) oversees registered warehouses and e-NWRs; food, consumer and trade ministries set many rules on farm goods.

Chapter 15

Commodities for you

Most people meet commodities as prices: petrol, cooking oil, gold. Some also want them in their savings. Here's how Kesarpur's families decide.

  • Gold as a small slice. Many advisers suggest gold as a modest part of savings, often around 5–10%, as a cushion against crises and a weak rupee, not as the main engine of growth. Rohit uses a gold ETF.
  • Jewellery for joy, not returns. Rukhsana still buys wedding jewellery, knowing that making charges and GST aren't an investment.
  • Avoid commodity futures unless you have a real exposure. Gopal's group hedges its crop; Kamala's silver bet was speculation with borrowed money.
  • Understand what a commodity fund actually holds. Physical gold, futures, or shares of mining companies all behave differently.
  • Remember commodities pay nothing. Over long periods, shares of good companies have usually grown far more than raw commodities, because companies reinvest and grow; a bar of gold doesn't.
Inflation hedge?Commodities often rise when inflation is driven by raw material costs, but the link is loose. Gold has gone years without beating inflation.
Schemes to avoid"Guaranteed return" commodity schemes, gold-savings plans from unregulated firms, and anything promising profits from "trading commodities for you".
Chapter 16

The next sowing

In October, Gopal's group sells its stored mustard to a large oil mill at ₹6,050 a quintal. After storage and interest, the 300 families earn about ₹2.6 lakh more than they would have in March. They use part of it to build their own small grading shed.

The mill presses the seed into the oil that goes into Sunrise's pickles. Rukhsana's daughter wears her new necklace at the wedding, with its HUID code checked on her brother Salim's phone. Harjeet watches the oil price on his phone between customers. And Iyer adds a new line to his bank's loan book: lending against warehouse receipts.

That is what a commodity is: a basic, interchangeable good that the world needs, whose price is pulled by seasons, weather, wars, interest rates and currencies. And that is what the commodity market is: the mandis, warehouses, exchanges and vaults where those goods and the promises about them are traded, so that what's grown or mined in one place and season can feed, power and adorn people in another.

After the story

Seven myths about commodities

Gold never falls.Gold fell sharply in 2013–2015 and was roughly flat for most of the 2010s.
Jewellery is the best way to own gold.Making charges and GST put you 10–20% behind from day one.
Holding crops always pays.Prices can fall after storage, and storage, interest and spoilage cost money.
A commodity fund tracks the spot price.Futures-based funds lose or gain from rolling contracts, so they can drift from spot.
Petrol prices follow crude one-for-one.Taxes and margins form a large share and are adjusted separately.
Digital gold on an app is the same as an ETF.An ETF is SEBI-regulated; app-based digital gold is not.
Prices can't go below zero.In April 2020, a US oil futures contract briefly did.

Before buying any commodity: a checklist

  • I know whether I'm buying for use, protection or speculation.
  • For gold jewellery, I've checked the hallmark and HUID, the making charges and the buy-back terms.
  • For investment gold, I've compared ETFs, gold funds and existing SGBs on cost and tax.
  • I'm using SEBI-regulated products or authorised dealers.
  • For stored crops, I've added up storage, interest, insurance and the risk that prices fall.
  • For any futures trade, I understand margin, leverage and delivery rules.
  • Commodities are a small part of my savings, not the whole plan.
