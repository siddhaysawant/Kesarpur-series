A story about the foreign exchange market, in sixteen chapters

The Kesarpur Remittance

Salim works in Dubai and sends 5,000 dirhams home to Kesarpur every month. How many rupees his mother receives depends on a price set every second by the largest market on Earth. Follow his money across the border, and you will understand how currencies are traded, why the rupee rises and falls, and who wins and loses when it does.

Part one

Money crossing borders

Prologue

Payday in Dubai

Salim grew up in Kesarpur, two streets from the bridge. For nine years he has worked as an electrician on building sites in Dubai. On the last day of every month, his salary of 7,500 UAE dirhams lands in his account. He keeps 2,500 for rent and food, and sends 5,000 home.

In Kesarpur, his mother Rukhsana runs the household on that money. She doesn't think in dirhams; she thinks in rupees. One month the transfer brings ₹1,14,000; a year later, the same 5,000 dirhams brings ₹1,22,000. She hasn't been sent more. The rupee has fallen.

That's good for her this month, but the same fall makes cooking oil, fertiliser and petrol in Kesarpur more expensive, because India buys them from abroad in dollars.

Behind every transfer, every import, every foreign holiday and every overseas investment, someone has to swap one currency for another. The place where that happens is the foreign exchange market, usually called forex or FX.

RemittancesMoney sent home by people working abroad. India receives more than any other country, over $100 billion a year in recent years, much of it from the Gulf, the US and the UK.
Chapter 1

Why money must be exchanged

Each country has its own money, and inside its borders, that's the only money that works. Rukhsana can't pay the grocer in dirhams. A Dubai supermarket won't accept rupees for Sunrise pickles. So whenever value crosses a border, one currency has to be sold and another bought.

Think of everyone in Kesarpur who touches another currency:

  • Salim has dirhams and wants rupees for his mother.
  • Lakshmi is paid in dollars by foreign supermarkets and needs rupees for wages and mangoes.
  • Imran, who runs the town's biggest phone shop, imports phones priced in dollars. He has rupees and needs dollars.
  • Kabir, Meera's great-grandson, is studying engineering in Melbourne. His family has rupees and needs Australian dollars for fees.
  • India's oil companies buy crude oil priced in dollars, so they need huge amounts of dollars every day.
  • Foreign investors buying Sunrise shares or Indian government bonds bring dollars and need rupees.

Some of these people have the currency others want. Salim and Lakshmi bring dollars and dirhams in; Imran, Kabir and the oil companies need dollars to send out. The forex market matches them, mostly through banks, and the price at which they swap is the exchange rate.

Why the dollarThe US dollar is on one side of almost 90% of all currency trades worldwide. Oil, many commodities and much world trade are priced in dollars, so most currencies are exchanged through it.
Biggest marketThe Bank for International Settlements' 2022 survey found about $7.5 trillion of currency traded on an average day, far more than all the world's stock markets combined.

Chapter 3

The journey of Salim's dirhams

On payday evening, Salim opens a remittance app on his phone and sends 5,000 dirhams. Ten minutes later, Rukhsana's phone buzzes in Kesarpur. It feels like magic, but several institutions passed the money along.

1. Exchange house

The Dubai exchange company takes his dirhams and offers a rate, say 22.80 rupees per dirham, a little below the 22.87 interbank rate. That gap is its main income.

2. Accounts abroad

The exchange house holds rupees in an account at an Indian bank (from its side, a nostro account: "ours, with you"). It doesn't ship money; it instructs the Indian bank to move rupees from that account.

3. Messaging

The instruction travels over secure bank messaging networks such as SWIFT, or a direct link between the exchange house and the Indian bank.

4. Last mile

The Indian bank credits Rukhsana's account through India's domestic payment systems, such as IMPS, in minutes.

Chapter 4

The price of a rupee

Why is a dollar worth ₹84, and not ₹40 or ₹200? For most of India's history since 1993, the answer has been the same as for mangoes: supply and demand, with the RBI stepping in to smooth sharp swings (Chapter 8).

On any day, dollars come into India from exporters, remittances and foreign investors. Dollars are needed by importers, students and travellers abroad, Indian companies investing overseas, and foreign investors taking money out. When more dollars are wanted than offered, buyers have to pay more rupees for each one, and the rupee weakens. When more dollars arrive than are needed, the rupee strengthens.

Floating rateA rate set by the market. India's rupee has floated, with RBI management, since 1993. Some currencies, like the UAE dirham, are pegged at a fixed rate to another.
Supply of dollarsWhen the rupee weakens, Indian exports become cheaper abroad and people abroad send more home, so more dollars arrive. That's why the supply line slopes upward.
Chapter 5

What moves the rupee

Behind the supply and demand of dollars are deeper forces. Iyer keeps a list on his desk of what he watches each morning.

Oil prices

India imports most of its crude oil, paid in dollars. When oil jumps from $70 to $100 a barrel, India needs many more dollars for the same oil, and the rupee usually weakens.

Interest rates

If Indian rates rise relative to US rates, foreign investors earn more by holding rupee bonds, so dollars flow in. When the US Federal Reserve raises rates sharply, money often leaves countries like India.

Inflation

If prices in India rise faster than in the US year after year, each rupee buys less, and over time the rupee tends to fall against the dollar. This idea is called purchasing power parity.

Foreign investment

Foreign companies building factories (FDI) and investors buying shares and bonds (FPI) bring dollars. FPI money can leave fast in a scare.

Trade and services

Exports of goods and especially services, like software and IT, bring dollars. A widening trade deficit weighs on the rupee.

Global mood and the dollar

When the world is frightened, investors rush to the dollar as a safe haven, and most emerging-market currencies, including the rupee, fall together, whatever is happening in India.

The masala dosa test

Kabir notices a plain masala dosa costs about ₹80 in Kesarpur and AUD 16 in Melbourne. At ₹55 per Australian dollar, the Melbourne dosa costs ₹880, eleven times as much. Does that mean the rupee is "too weak"? Not exactly. Rent, wages and services are far cheaper in India, and they can't be traded across borders. Comparisons like this, made famous by The Economist's Big Mac index, show why exchange rates can differ a lot from what local prices would suggest, and why economists adjust incomes for purchasing power when comparing countries.

Chapter 6

Winners and losers

"Is a weak rupee good or bad?" Kabir's father asks Iyer. Iyer says it depends entirely on which side of the border your money is on. Move the slider and watch what happens to each person in Kesarpur.

PersonForeign amountIn rupeesChange vs ₹84

A weaker rupee helps people who receive foreign money: Salim's family, exporters like Lakshmi, IT companies, and tourism. It hurts people who pay foreign money: importers like Imran, students abroad like Kabir, and, through oil and imported goods, every household, because petrol, cooking oil, electronics and fertiliser get dearer. That spreads into inflation.

A stronger rupee does the reverse. That's why neither "strong" nor "weak" is simply good. What hurts most is a sudden move that nobody planned for.

DepreciationA fall in a floating currency's value. A devaluation is a deliberate cut by a government in a fixed or pegged rate.
Imported inflationWhen the rupee falls, the rupee price of imports rises, and some of that passes on to shop prices. The RBI watches this closely.
Part two

The country's view

Chapter 7

India's foreign accounts

Add up every dollar that comes into India and every dollar that goes out, and you get the balance of payments. It's like a household's account of money from abroad, but for the whole country. Here is an illustrative month.

Current account

Trade in goods and services, plus income and transfers like Salim's. India usually imports more goods than it exports, partly offset by strong services exports and remittances. The result is often a small current account deficit.

Capital account

Investment and borrowing: foreign companies building factories, investors buying shares and bonds, Indian companies borrowing abroad. These flows usually cover the current account deficit.

Reserves

If more comes in than goes out, the RBI typically buys the extra dollars and adds them to foreign exchange reserves. In a bad month, it sells some. India's reserves are among the largest in the world.

Chapter 8

The RBI in the market

One morning, a global scare sends foreign investors rushing to sell Indian shares and take their dollars home. The rupee falls from 84 to 85.50 by noon. Importers panic and buy dollars in case it falls further, which pushes it down more.

Then large sell orders for dollars appear from a few public-sector banks. Dealers know what that usually means: the RBI is selling dollars from its reserves through those banks. The fall slows, and the rupee closes at 85.10.

India runs a managed float. The RBI doesn't defend a particular rate, but it steps in to prevent disorderly swings: selling dollars when the rupee falls too fast, buying them when it rises too fast. Choose a direction and watch it act.

Intervention has side effects. When the RBI sells dollars, banks pay it rupees, which drains rupee cash from the banking system (the money market story's "defending the rupee" day). The RBI often offsets that with other operations, called sterilisation. And reserves aren't unlimited: a central bank that tries to hold a rate against the whole market can run out, as several did in the crises of Chapter 13.

Forex reservesDollars, other currencies, gold and other assets held by the central bank. They pay for imports in a crisis and give confidence that the country can meet its foreign payments.
Smoothing, not fixingThe RBI's stated aim is to curb excessive volatility, not to target a particular level of the rupee.
Chapter 9

Tomorrow's exchange rate

Lakshmi will be paid $10 lakh by a Dubai supermarket in three months. In the derivatives story, her bank offered to lock in ₹84.40 per dollar. Why not ₹84, today's rate?

Iyer explains with an arbitrage argument, the same logic as futures pricing in the derivatives story. A bank that promises Lakshmi rupees in three months could borrow dollars today, sell them for rupees at ₹84, and invest those rupees for three months. Rupee interest rates (say 6.5%) are higher than dollar rates (say 4.5%), so the rupees grow faster than the dollar debt. The fair forward rate passes that difference on.

Forward = Spot × (1 + rupee rate × t) ÷ (1 + dollar rate × t) 3 months: 84.00 × (1 + 0.065 × 0.25) ÷ (1 + 0.045 × 0.25) ≈ 84.42 1 year: 84.00 × 1.065 ÷ 1.045 ≈ 85.61
Interest rate parityThe rule that the forward premium should roughly equal the interest rate difference between two currencies. Otherwise, banks could make riskless profits.
Spot settlementA normal "spot" forex trade settles two working days later (T+2). Forwards settle on any agreed future date.
Chapter 10

Protecting against the rupee

After a bad year when the rupee jumped from 84 to 90, Imran lost ₹18 lakh on one shipment of phones. His dollar bill was the same, but in rupees it cost much more, and he had already fixed his shop prices. He came to Iyer for help. So did everyone else.

In India, companies with real foreign-currency exposure can hedge through banks with forwards, swaps and options. Anyone can also trade exchange-traded currency futures and options on rupee pairs on Indian exchanges, like those in the derivatives story. The principle is the same as Gopal's mango contract: give up some possible gain to remove a damaging loss.

Natural hedgeMatching foreign income with foreign costs in the same currency, so currency moves largely cancel out without any contract.
Unhedged borrowingCompanies that borrow cheaply in dollars but earn only rupees take a large hidden risk. If the rupee falls sharply, their debt grows in rupee terms overnight.
Part three

The world's biggest market

Chapter 11

The market that never sleeps

The forex market has no single building or exchange. It's a global web of banks, dealers and electronic platforms trading with each other directly (over the counter). Because it spans every time zone, it runs 24 hours a day on weekdays, passing from Asia to Europe to America and back.

The interbank market

The core: big global banks quoting prices to each other and to large clients in huge amounts. A few financial centres, especially London, New York, Singapore and Hong Kong, handle most of it.

Who trades

Banks, companies paying for trade, investment funds, central banks, hedge funds, high-speed trading firms, and, through layers of brokers, individuals. Most volume is between financial institutions, not for trade in goods.

India's onshore market

Authorised banks trade rupee pairs with each other and with clients, mainly during Indian business hours. Exchanges also list rupee futures and options (USD/INR futures began on NSE in 2008).

Offshore NDF market

Outside India, in places like Singapore and London, investors trade non-deliverable forwards on the rupee, settled in dollars. Prices there often move first when news breaks overnight.

Spot, forward, swap

Most daily turnover globally is in FX swaps (exchanging currencies now and reversing later), followed by spot and outright forwards. Options are a smaller share.

Pips and paise

Currency prices move in tiny steps. For EUR/USD the smallest standard step, a pip, is 0.0001. For USD/INR, quotes move in quarters of a paisa.

Chapter 12

A short life of the rupee

Meera, now in her nineties, remembers when a dollar cost under ₹10. The rupee's history mirrors India's economic story.

Before 1991, the rupee's value was set by the government, and buying foreign currency was tightly controlled. In 1991, India nearly ran out of dollars: reserves fell to a few weeks of imports, and the government had to pledge gold abroad to borrow. It devalued the rupee sharply and began opening the economy. By 1993, the rupee was moving to a market-determined rate.

Since then, the rupee has drifted weaker over the long run, mostly because India's inflation has been higher than America's, with sharper drops in crises: 2008, 2013 (the "taper tantrum", when the rupee fell to a record low of nearly ₹69 after the US hinted at slowing its bond buying), and 2022, when the US raised rates fast.

1991's lessonA country that runs short of foreign currency can't pay for oil, medicine or debt. That's why India has built up large reserves since.
Long-run driftA currency losing value against the dollar over decades isn't necessarily a sign of failure. It largely reflects the inflation gap, while India's economy grew many times larger.
Chapter 13

Currency crises

Currency markets can move with a violence that surprises even professionals, especially when a government promises to hold a rate that the market no longer believes.

Britain, 1992The pound was held within a band against other European currencies. Speculators, most famously George Soros's fund, bet heavily that Britain couldn't keep it there. On "Black Wednesday", the Bank of England spent billions and raised rates, then gave up and let the pound fall. Soros's fund reportedly made about $1 billion.
Asia, 1997Thailand had pegged the baht to the dollar while its companies borrowed heavily in dollars. When investors fled, reserves ran out, the peg broke, and the baht collapsed. The panic spread to Indonesia, South Korea and Malaysia, where companies with dollar debts were ruined overnight.
India, 2013When the US Federal Reserve hinted it would slow its bond buying, money rushed out of emerging markets. The rupee fell about 15% in a few months. The RBI raised short-term rates, restricted gold imports, and offered banks a special window to attract foreign currency deposits from Indians abroad, which brought in over $30 billion.
The common threadA fixed or defended rate, large borrowing in a foreign currency, a widening deficit, and money that can leave quickly. When investors decide the rate can't hold, they all try to get out at once.
Speculative attackMassive selling of a currency by investors betting a peg will break. If reserves run low, the bet becomes self-fulfilling.
The trilemmaA country can't have all three at once: a fixed exchange rate, free movement of capital, and its own interest rate policy. It must give up one.
Chapter 14

The rules in India

Foreign exchange in India is governed by the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999, which replaced a far stricter older law, and by RBI rules under it.

  • Authorised dealers. Only banks and firms authorised by the RBI may deal in foreign currency with the public. Money changers need authorisation too.
  • The Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS). A resident individual can send up to $2,50,000 a year abroad for permitted purposes: study, travel, medical care, gifts, and investing abroad. Tax may be collected at source on some remittances above a threshold, which can be adjusted against your tax.
  • Current and capital accounts. The rupee is freely convertible for trade and current payments, but some capital transactions, like certain overseas investments and foreign borrowing, have limits.
  • Retail forex trading. Residents may trade currency derivatives only in permitted pairs involving the rupee, on recognised Indian exchanges or with authorised banks. Online platforms offering leveraged trading in pairs like EUR/USD or "forex signals" to Indian residents are not authorised, and the RBI publishes an Alert List of such unauthorised forex trading platforms. Sending money to them can break the law and is a common route to fraud.
Warning signsSocial media ads promising daily forex profits, payments to personal UPI IDs, "account managers" on messaging apps, and pressure to deposit more to "unlock" withdrawals.
Check before you tradeIs the platform authorised in India? Is it on the RBI Alert List? Is the pair a rupee pair on a recognised exchange?
Chapter 15

Forex in your own life

Most people meet the forex market a few times a year: a trip, a fee, a gift, an online purchase. The same principles apply.

Travelling

  • Compare the total rupees you pay, not the rate shown on a board; fees can hide in the rate.
  • Prepaid forex cards and some international debit or credit cards often beat airport counters.
  • When a shop or ATM abroad offers to charge you "in rupees", decline. This is dynamic currency conversion, and its rate is usually poor. Pay in the local currency.

Sending money home or abroad

  • Compare the amount that actually arrives across a few services.
  • Regular smaller transfers average out rate swings; trying to time the "best day" rarely works.

Studying abroad

  • Fees are fixed in foreign currency, so a falling rupee raises your cost. Budget with a margin of 5–10%.
  • Education loans, LRS limits and tax collected at source all have rules worth checking early.

Investing abroad

Rohit puts a small part of his savings into an international index fund. If the rupee weakens, his foreign investments are worth more in rupees, which partly protects him. That's also a currency risk in reverse: if the rupee strengthens, it reduces his returns.

Total costRate margin + fees + card charges. The rate closest to the interbank (mid-market) rate isn't always cheapest if fees are high.
Gifts from abroadMoney from close relatives abroad is generally allowed and often tax-free, but rules depend on amount and relationship. Keep records.
Chapter 16

Eid at home

Three years later, Salim comes home for Eid. He has sent 5,000 dirhams every month without fail. Some months they turned into ₹1,10,000 and some into ₹1,23,000, depending on oil prices, US interest rates and the mood of investors he'd never meet. With help from Rukhsana's careful budgeting, the money paid for his sister's wedding and a new roof.

Lakshmi's export orders are hedged three months ahead. Imran buys his dollars forward and sleeps better. Kabir has graduated, and his family is glad they paid fees in stages.

That is what an exchange rate is: the price of one country's money in another's. And that is what the forex market is: a never-closing global marketplace that swaps the world's currencies, so that a nurse can attend a conference, an electrician can support his mother, a pickle maker can sell abroad, and a country can buy the oil it runs on.

After the story

Seven myths about forex

A weak rupee means a weak economy.It reflects inflation, interest rates, oil and global flows. India has grown fast while the rupee drifted weaker.
A strong rupee is always good.It hurts exporters, IT companies and families receiving remittances.
The forward rate is a forecast.It mostly reflects the interest rate gap, not a prediction.
"Zero commission" means free.The cost is often hidden in a worse exchange rate.
Paying in rupees abroad is safer.Dynamic currency conversion usually gives a poor rate.
Online forex trading apps are a legal way to earn.Leveraged trading in non-rupee pairs on unauthorised platforms isn't permitted for Indian residents, and many are scams.
The RBI fixes the rupee.It smooths sharp moves; the market sets the level.

Before any currency transaction: a checklist

  • I've compared the amount actually received or paid across a few providers.
  • I know the mid-market rate, so I can see the margin.
  • I've included every fee and card charge.
  • I'll pay abroad in local currency, not rupees.
  • For a big future payment, I've considered locking the rate or paying in stages.
  • I'm using an authorised bank or dealer.
  • Any trading platform is authorised in India and not on the RBI Alert List.
  • I know the LRS limit and any tax collected at source.

Glossary

Appreciation / depreciation
A floating currency gaining / losing value.
Authorised dealer
A bank or firm permitted by the RBI to deal in foreign exchange.
Balance of payments
Record of all a country's transactions with the rest of the world.
Base / quote currency
In USD/INR, USD is priced in INR.
Bid / ask
Price a dealer pays / charges.
Cross rate
A rate between two currencies worked out through a third.
Current account deficit
Spending more abroad on goods, services and income than earning.
Devaluation
An official cut in a fixed or pegged rate.
Dynamic currency conversion
Being charged abroad in your home currency, usually at a poor rate.
FEMA
India's Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999.
Forward premium
How much the forward rate exceeds spot.
Forex reserves
Foreign currency and gold held by the central bank.
FX swap
Exchange currencies now and reverse the exchange later.
Hedging
Reducing the risk of a currency move.
Interest rate parity
Forward premium ≈ interest rate difference.
Intervention
A central bank buying or selling currency in the market.
LRS
Liberalised Remittance Scheme: up to $2,50,000 a year abroad per resident.
Managed float
Market-set rate with central bank smoothing.
Mid-market rate
Halfway between interbank bid and ask.
NDF
Non-deliverable forward, traded offshore and settled in dollars.
Nostro / vostro
"Our account with you" / "your account with us", between banks.
Peg
A currency fixed to another at a set rate.
Pip
The smallest standard price step in a currency pair.
Purchasing power parity
The idea that rates adjust so the same goods cost the same everywhere.
Remittance
Money sent across borders, often by workers to family.
Spot rate
Rate for immediate exchange, settled in two working days.
Sterilisation
Offsetting the effect of intervention on domestic money supply.
