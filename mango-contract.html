Part one

Agreeing on tomorrow's price

Prologue

The gamble in the orchard

Gopal owns a mango orchard twelve kilometres outside Kesarpur. Every January the trees flower, and every May he harvests about 10,000 kg of fruit. Between flowering and harvest, he spends about ₹4 lakh on labour, fertiliser, water and spraying, much of it borrowed.

He has no idea what his mangoes will sell for. In a good year for everyone, with heavy crops across the state, prices can crash to ₹40 a kilo. In a year of unseasonal rain or heat, they can shoot to ₹90. The same orchard, the same work, can earn ₹4 lakh or ₹9 lakh. At ₹40, he can barely repay his loan. Two bad years in a row, and he could lose the land his grandfather planted.

Twelve kilometres away, Lakshmi has the opposite problem. Sunrise Foods, the pickle company from the stock market story, needs those mangoes. If prices jump to ₹90, her costs explode and her profit vanishes, because she can't raise the price of pickle jars already promised to supermarkets. She is afraid of high prices. Gopal is afraid of low prices.

Both of them are gambling on a price neither can control. And neither of them wants to.

Fourth storyThis follows The Kesarpur Bridge Bond, The Common Pot and The Pickle Share. Kamala, who lost money on options in the stock story, explains what happened in Chapter 12.
Price riskThe danger that a price moves against you before you buy or sell. Farmers, factories, airlines, importers, exporters and investors all live with it.
Chapter 1

The handshake

In January, Lakshmi drives out to the orchard. Over tea, they agree on something simple: in May, Gopal will deliver 10,000 kg of grade-A mangoes to Sunrise, and Sunrise will pay ₹60 a kilo, whatever the market price is then. No money changes hands today. They write it on one page and both sign.

This is a forward contract: an agreement made today to buy or sell something on a future date at a price fixed today.

Notice three things. First, with the contract, both of them know their number in January: ₹6 lakh. They can plan, borrow, and sleep. Second, the contract's gain for one is always exactly the other's loss. If May's price is ₹80, Gopal "gives up" ₹2 lakh he could have earned, and Sunrise "saves" exactly ₹2 lakh. Third, and most important: neither of them was trying to make money from the contract. They were trying to remove uncertainty. That is called hedging.

Gopal's neighbour mocks him in a year when prices hit ₹85. "You lost ₹2.5 lakh!" Gopal doesn't see it that way. He bought certainty. In the year prices fell to ₹42, the same neighbour had to sell two acres.

Forward contractA private agreement to trade at a fixed future price and date. Custom terms, no money upfront, settled at the end.
HedgingTaking a position that offsets a risk you already have. A hedger gives up some possible gain to avoid a possible loss.
Long and shortThe buyer (Sunrise) is long: gains if the price rises. The seller (Gopal) is short: gains if the price falls.
Chapter 2

What a derivative is

The paper Gopal and Lakshmi signed has no value of its own. Its value depends entirely on something else: the price of mangoes. If mangoes rise, the contract becomes valuable to Sunrise. If they fall, it becomes valuable to Gopal. Its value is derived from the mango price. That is why it is called a derivative.

The thing a derivative depends on is the underlying. It can be almost anything with a price: mangoes, wheat, gold, crude oil, a company's share, a stock index like the Nifty, a currency like the dollar, an interest rate, even the chance that a company defaults.

There are four basic kinds, and this story covers each:

Every derivative has two sides, and before costs it is a zero-sum game: what one side gains, the other loses. That's different from owning a share in Sunrise, where if the company grows, every shareholder can gain at once. A derivative creates no new value by itself. What it can do is move risk from someone who doesn't want it to someone who is willing to carry it, for a price.

Three kinds of people use derivatives:

  • Hedgers like Gopal and Lakshmi, who already have a risk and want to reduce it.
  • Speculators, who have no mangoes and no pickle factory, but take a position because they think the price will move. They carry the risk hedgers don't want, and they make it easier for hedgers to find someone to trade with.
  • Arbitrageurs, who look for the same thing trading at two different prices, buy the cheap one and sell the expensive one, and in doing so keep prices in line.
UnderlyingThe asset or rate whose price a derivative depends on. No underlying, no derivative.
Very old ideaRice futures traded at the Dojima exchange in Osaka from the 1730s. The Chicago Board of Trade was founded in 1848 so farmers and grain merchants could fix prices ahead of harvest, much like Gopal and Lakshmi.
Chapter 3

From handshake to exchange

The handshake works well for Gopal and Lakshmi, who trust each other. But three problems appear as soon as more people try it.

  • Counterparty risk. In a year prices hit ₹85, a farmer in the next village quietly sold his mangoes to a trader at ₹85 instead of delivering to his buyer at ₹60. The contract was only as good as his word. And in a year prices crashed to ₹40, a small pickle maker simply refused to pay ₹60.
  • Hard to find a partner. Gopal found Lakshmi by luck. A farmer who wants to lock in a price may not know any buyer who wants the same quantity on the same date.
  • No way out. If Gopal's trees are hit by disease in March, he's stuck with a contract to deliver mangoes he doesn't have.

A futures contract solves all three. It is a forward with three changes:

  • Standardised. The exchange fixes everything except the price: the quantity (say, one contract = 1,000 kg), the quality, the delivery place, and the expiry date. Every contract is identical, so anyone can trade with anyone.
  • Traded on an exchange. Buyers and sellers meet in an order book, as with shares. You can buy or sell a contract any day before expiry, which also means you can exit by doing the opposite trade.
  • Guaranteed by a clearing house. The clearing corporation becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. Gopal no longer needs to trust Lakshmi; he needs to trust the clearing house, which protects itself with margin (Chapter 4).

Most futures today don't end with a truck of mangoes. Before expiry, most traders close their position by taking the opposite trade, and simply collect or pay the difference in price. Many contracts, including index futures, are cash-settled: there's nothing to deliver, so only the difference is paid. In India, futures on individual shares are physically settled: if you hold to expiry, you actually buy or sell the shares.

Forward vs. futureForward: private, custom, one counterparty, settled at the end. Future: exchange-traded, standard, clearing-house guaranteed, settled every day.
Lot sizeThe fixed quantity in one contract. You can trade one lot, two lots, but not half a lot. SEBI sets minimum contract values for equity derivatives.
ExpiryThe last day a contract trades. Index and stock futures in India expire monthly on a fixed weekday, with up to three months' contracts trading at once.
Chapter 4

Margin and the daily settling-up

How does the clearing house make sure nobody walks away? Two tools: margin and mark-to-market.

Rohit, the young engineer from the stock market story, buys one index futures contract. The index stands at 20,000 and one contract covers 75 units, so the contract is worth 20,000 × 75 = ₹15 lakh. He doesn't pay ₹15 lakh. He deposits margin, about 12% of it, ₹1.8 lakh, with his broker. Margin is not a price or a fee. It's a security deposit to show he can cover losses.

Then, every evening, the exchange settles up. If the index rose, money is moved from sellers' accounts into buyers'. If it fell, the reverse. This is mark-to-market: losses can't pile up quietly until expiry, because they are paid every single day.

On days 2 and 3, Rohit's broker sent him a margin call: top up your account by the end of the day, or we'll close your position. He paid. Had he not, his contract would have been sold at the day-3 low, locking in a ₹33,750 loss, and he would never have seen the recovery.

This daily discipline is what makes futures safe for the system. Nobody can lose more than a day or two of price moves before they have to pay. It is not what makes them safe for the trader. That's the next chapter.

MarginA deposit, set by the exchange based on how much the price could move in a bad day or two. More volatile underlyings need higher margins.
Mark-to-marketRevaluing every open position at the day's closing price and settling gains and losses in cash each day.
Margin callA demand to add money when losses eat into your margin. Failing to pay means the broker closes your position, often at the worst moment.
Chapter 5

Leverage cuts both ways

Look again at Rohit's trade. The index rose 1.5% (20,000 to 20,300). His profit was ₹22,500 on ₹1.8 lakh of margin: 12.5%. A small move in the underlying became a big move in his account. That multiplier is leverage.

With ₹1.8 lakh of margin controlling ₹15 lakh of index, Rohit's leverage is about 8×. Every 1% move in the index is about an 8% move in his money, in either direction.

Leverage8.3×
Gain or loss−₹75,000
On your margin−41.7%

A 5% fall in the index, which happens several times in many years, wipes out over 40% of Rohit's money. A 12% fall wipes out all of it. If the index gaps down overnight more than that, he can lose more than his deposit and still owe the broker.

Compare this with buying ₹1.8 lakh of an index fund. A 5% fall costs ₹9,000, and he can wait as long as he likes for a recovery. With the futures contract, margin calls may force him out before any recovery comes.

LeverageControlling a large position with a small amount of money. It doesn't change the odds of a trade; it multiplies the result, good or bad.
For a hedgerLeverage is useful: Lakshmi can protect ₹6 lakh of mango purchases while tying up only a fraction of that in margin. The loss on her futures is offset by cheaper mangoes, so her overall risk falls.
Chapter 6

What a future should cost

Rohit notices that the index futures price is not 20,000 like the index itself. It's about 20,107. Why pay more for something later than now?

Neha, his analyst friend, explains with an arbitrage argument. There are two ways to own the index in 30 days:

  • Buy the shares today for 20,000, paying for them now. That money could have earned interest instead, at about 6.5% a year.
  • Buy a future and keep the money in the bank for 30 days. You earn interest, and pay for the index only at expiry.

Both give you the index in 30 days, so they must cost the same in the end. The future must therefore cost about the spot price plus the interest you'd earn by waiting, minus any dividends you'd miss by not holding the shares. This is the cost of carry.

Fair futures price ≈ Spot × (1 + r × days ÷ 365) − dividends before expiry = 20,000 × (1 + 0.065 × 30 ÷ 365) ≈ 20,107 (ignoring dividends)

If the future traded at 20,300, arbitrageurs would buy the shares, sell the future, and lock in a near-riskless profit until the gap closed. If it traded at 19,900, they'd do the reverse. That's why futures stay close to fair value. The gap between the future and the spot price is called the basis. It shrinks every day, and on expiry day the future equals the spot. This is called convergence.

For mangoes, cost of carry also includes storage, spoilage and insurance. For crops that can't be stored long, futures prices before and after a harvest can differ hugely, because they depend on expected supply, not on today's fruit.

Contango and backwardationFutures above spot is contango, the normal case. Futures below spot is backwardation, often seen when something is scarce right now.
Price discoveryFutures markets often react to news before the spot market does, so they help set prices for the real goods.
Part two

The right, not the obligation

Chapter 7

Options: insurance for prices

After a few years of forwards, Gopal has a complaint. When prices fall, the contract saves him. But in the year prices hit ₹85, he watched his neighbours earn ₹25 a kilo more than him. Is there a way to be protected from low prices without giving up high ones?

There is, but it costs money. A trader at the district mandi offers him a deal: for ₹3 a kilo paid today, Gopal gets the right to sell his mangoes to the trader in May at ₹55 a kilo. If the market price in May is below ₹55, Gopal uses that right. If it's above, he ignores it and sells at the market price. The trader keeps the ₹3 either way.

This is an option. Specifically, it's a put option: the right to sell at a fixed price. The fixed price, ₹55, is the strike price. The ₹3 is the premium.

The put is exactly like crop insurance, or car insurance. You pay a premium. If disaster strikes, you're protected. If it doesn't, the premium is gone, and you're glad you didn't need it.

Lakshmi can do the mirror image. She buys a call option: the right to buy mangoes at ₹65 in May. If prices go to ₹90, she pays only ₹65. If they fall to ₹45, she ignores the option and buys cheaply at the market.

There's one crucial asymmetry. The buyer of an option has a right and can walk away; the most they can lose is the premium. The seller (also called the writer) has an obligation. The trader who sold Gopal the put must buy his mangoes at ₹55 even if the market collapses to ₹30. The seller collects a small premium and takes on a potentially large loss. Option sellers are the insurance companies of the derivatives world.

Call and putCall: the right to buy at the strike. Gains when prices rise. Put: the right to sell at the strike. Gains when prices fall.
ExerciseUsing the right. In India, stock and index options are European-style: they can be exercised only at expiry. American-style options can be exercised any time before.
Buyer vs. sellerBuyer: limited loss (the premium), possibly large gain. Seller: limited gain (the premium), possibly large loss.

Options are described by where the strike sits relative to today's price. A call with a strike below the current price is in the money: it would be worth exercising now. At the current price, it's at the money. Above, it's out of the money: it needs the price to rise before it's worth anything. For puts, it's the reverse.

Chapter 9

What makes an option expensive

Why did the ₹190 call cost ₹2.82 and not ₹1 or ₹10? In 1973, the economists Fischer Black and Myron Scholes, with Robert Merton, published a formula that answered this question. It earned a Nobel prize in 1997, and versions of it are still used by every options desk.

You don't need the formula, but you do need its ingredients. An option's premium has two parts:

  • Intrinsic value: what it would be worth if exercised right now. A ₹170 call when the share is at ₹180 has ₹10 of intrinsic value. Out-of-the-money options have none.
  • Time value: everything else. It's the price of the chance that the option becomes more valuable before expiry.

Time value depends mostly on two things: how much time is left, and how much the price is expected to swing. Try them.

₹190 call premium₹2.82
₹170 put premium₹2.05
Call loses per day (theta)₹0.07

Sunrise share at ₹180, interest rate 6.5%. Both options are out of the money, so their premium is entirely time value.

Slide days to expiry down to 1, and the call is worth almost nothing: there's no time left for the share to climb to ₹190. Time value melts every day, faster near expiry. This is time decay, and it works against every option buyer and for every seller.

Slide volatility up, and both options become more expensive. If the share might swing wildly, the chance of reaching ₹190 rises. The volatility that the market's option prices imply is called implied volatility. It jumps before results, elections and budgets, and in crashes, because that's when people pay most for protection.

Traders summarise how an option reacts to each ingredient using the Greeks:

MoneynessIn the money options cost more but behave more like the underlying. Far out of the money options are cheap because they usually expire worthless.
Cheap isn't cheapA ₹0.40 option isn't a bargain. Its low price reflects a low chance of paying off. Slide days to 7 to see a weekly ₹190 call.
Part three

Protection and peril

Chapter 10

Hedging in real life

Derivatives were invented for hedging, and most of their value to the economy still comes from it. Here is how each character in the Kesarpur stories uses them to reduce risk.

Gopal: a floor for his crop

Buys a put on mangoes at ₹55 for ₹3/kg. His worst case is ₹52 a kilo, and he keeps the upside. In India, exchanges list futures on several farm goods; farmer groups (FPOs) can hedge together to reach lot sizes.

Lakshmi: a ceiling on costs

Buys mango forwards from farmers and calls for the rest, and buys edible oil futures. Her pickle margins no longer swing with the weather.

Rohit: protecting shares

Owns 1,000 Sunrise shares at ₹180 before a risky results day. He buys ₹170 puts for ₹2.05 a share, ₹2,050 in total. Whatever happens, his shares are effectively worth at least ₹167.95 until expiry. This is a protective put.

Rohit: earning from shares

In a quiet month, he sells ₹190 calls on the same shares for ₹2.82, earning ₹2,820. If Sunrise rises above ₹190, he must sell at ₹190 and misses gains above that. This is a covered call: income in exchange for capping upside.

Ananya's mutual fund

Fund managers sell index futures to reduce exposure quickly without selling dozens of shares, then buy them back later. Arbitrage funds (from the fund story) earn low-risk returns from the gap between share and futures prices.

A hedge is not free

Premiums, margins and missed gains are the cost. A perfect hedge removes both losses and gains. The goal isn't to make money; it's to make the outcome survivable.

Chapter 11

Swaps and currency contracts

The council's interest rate swap

Years after the bridge, Farida, the Kesarpur treasurer, borrows ₹20 crore from a bank for a water treatment plant. The loan has a floating rate: it resets every year to the central bank's policy rate plus 2%. Today that's 8.5%. If rates rise to 11%, the town's interest bill jumps by ₹50 lakh a year, money it doesn't have.

Farida signs an interest rate swap with another bank. Each year, the town pays the bank a fixed 8.5% on ₹20 crore. The bank pays the town whatever the floating rate is. The town passes that floating payment straight to its lender.

Only the interest is exchanged; the ₹20 crore itself never changes hands. That ₹20 crore is called the notional amount. Interest rate swaps are by far the biggest derivative market in the world by notional value, used by banks, companies and governments to manage borrowing costs.

Sunrise's dollar problem

Sunrise sells $10 lakh (one million dollars) of pickle to a supermarket chain in Dubai, to be paid in three months. Today a dollar buys ₹84. If the rupee strengthens to ₹80 by payment day, Sunrise receives ₹40 lakh less than planned, enough to wipe out the profit on the order.

Its bank offers a currency forward: Sunrise agrees today to sell $10 lakh in three months at ₹84.40 per dollar. (The forward rate is a little higher than today's because Indian interest rates are higher than US rates, the same cost-of-carry logic as Chapter 6.) Sunrise now knows it will receive ₹8.44 crore, whether the rupee goes to ₹80 or ₹87.

SwapAn exchange of cash-flow streams: fixed for floating interest, one currency for another, or a commodity price for a fixed price.
Who regulatesIn India, interest rate and currency derivatives are mainly regulated by the RBI, and exchange-traded equity and commodity derivatives by SEBI.
Counterparty risk returnsMost swaps are private (OTC) deals. If the bank on the other side fails, the protection can vanish. After 2008, regulators pushed many swaps through central clearing houses.
Chapter 12

Kamala's lottery tickets

Kamala, Rohit's great-aunt, mentioned her options losses in the stock market story. Here is what happened.

A video on her phone showed a young man who "turned ₹10,000 into ₹4 lakh" buying index options on expiry day. It looked easy. Options that cost only a few rupees sometimes jumped to ₹100 in a few hours. Kamala opened an account and started buying cheap, out-of-the-money weekly calls and puts, a few thousand rupees at a time.

The first month, she doubled her money twice. Then came a long string of small losses: most of her options expired worthless, a few rupees at a time. When she was down, she bought more, to win it back. Over a year she lost ₹3.2 lakh, most of her savings outside her house.

She was not unlucky. She was on the wrong side of the arithmetic.

  • Time decay. Every option she bought lost value every day it didn't move her way. Weekly options decay fastest.
  • Cheap options are cheap for a reason. A far out-of-the-money option priced at ₹2 is pricing a small chance of a big move. Most expire at zero.
  • Costs. Brokerage, transaction taxes, exchange fees and the bid–ask spread were charged on every trade, win or lose. Frequent trading turned small costs into large ones.
  • The other side. The people selling her options were often professional firms with fast computers, careful risk models and diversified books.
  • Behaviour. Chasing losses, betting bigger after wins, and trading on feelings.

Her story is common. SEBI studied individual traders in equity futures and options and found that about 93% of them lost money over the three years to March 2024, with total net losses of over ₹1.8 lakh crore. Earlier studies found similar results. In response, from late 2024 SEBI raised minimum contract sizes, limited weekly expiries to one index per exchange, and required option premiums to be paid upfront, to make reckless betting harder.

Selling options isn't safe either. Kamala's neighbour, who sold far out-of-the-money options to collect "steady income", earned small amounts for months and then lost two years' gains in a single day when the market gapped. Traders describe it as picking up coins in front of a steamroller.

Speculation is legalSpeculators provide a real service by carrying risk hedgers want to shed. But they are paid only if they are better informed, better capitalised or more disciplined than the other side.
Risk disclosureIndian brokers must show a risk disclosure, based on SEBI's study, before you trade F&O. Read it.
Chapter 13

When derivatives blew up

In 2002 Warren Buffett called derivatives "financial weapons of mass destruction". He used them himself, but he was warning about what happens when leverage, complexity and weak controls come together. History gives examples at every scale.

Barings Bank, 1995A single trader in Singapore, Nick Leeson, built up huge unauthorised futures positions on the Japanese stock market and hid the losses in a secret account. An earthquake in Kobe sent the market down, losses reached about £800 million, and Britain's oldest merchant bank, more than 200 years old, collapsed.
LTCM, 1998A US hedge fund run by star traders and two Nobel prize-winning economists used enormous leverage on trades its models said were nearly riskless. When Russia defaulted and markets behaved in ways the models didn't expect, the fund lost billions in weeks. Banks were organised to rescue it to stop the damage spreading.
2008 financial crisisBanks had packaged US home loans into complex securities and sold protection on them using credit default swaps, insurance-like contracts against default. The insurer AIG had sold vast amounts without setting aside enough to pay. When home loans defaulted, AIG needed a huge government rescue, and the web of OTC contracts spread panic across the global financial system.
Small-scale, every dayIndividuals wiped out by leveraged positions, companies that "hedged" by taking bets bigger than their real exposure, and exporters ruined by complex currency products they didn't understand.

The common thread isn't the derivative. It's leverage (positions far bigger than the capital behind them), opacity (nobody knew who owed what to whom), and overconfidence (models that assumed rare events wouldn't happen). The fixes since 2008, more central clearing, more margin, reporting of OTC trades, and tighter limits for individuals, target exactly those three.

Credit default swapA contract where one side pays a regular fee and the other pays out if a borrower defaults. Like insurance on a bond, but you don't need to own the bond to buy it.
Systemic riskThe danger that one firm's failure spreads to others because they're linked by contracts. Clearing houses reduce it but concentrate risk in themselves, so they are heavily regulated.
Chapter 14

The derivatives market

Zoom out from Kesarpur. The derivatives market is where all these contracts are created and traded: on exchanges and privately, on almost every price in the economy. It's enormous. The notional value of outstanding contracts worldwide is many times the size of the world's economy, though the money actually at risk is far smaller, because notional is just the size of the underlying, not what changes hands.

Exchange-traded

Standard futures and options on exchanges such as NSE, BSE, MCX and NCDEX in India, or CME in the US. Cleared centrally, margined daily, prices public. Equity index options dominate Indian volumes; by number of contracts traded, NSE has been the world's largest derivatives exchange in recent years.

Over the counter (OTC)

Private, customised contracts between banks, companies and funds: swaps, forwards, complex options. Far larger by notional value worldwide. Since 2008, many are reported to trade repositories and cleared centrally.

Underlyings in India

Equity: Nifty, Bank Nifty, Sensex and about 200 stocks. Currency: rupee against the dollar, euro, pound and yen. Interest rates: government bond futures and swaps. Commodities: gold, silver, crude oil, natural gas, metals and some farm goods.

Participants

Banks and companies hedging; mutual funds and insurers managing risk; foreign investors; proprietary trading firms and market makers, many using algorithms; and a large number of individual traders.

Regulators

SEBI for exchange-traded equity, commodity and currency derivatives; RBI for interest rate and OTC currency derivatives and for banks. Exchanges and clearing corporations set margins and position limits.

Data traders watch

Open interest: the number of contracts still open. Put–call ratio: puts traded vs. calls, a mood gauge. Implied volatility indices such as India VIX, sometimes called the fear gauge.

A short Indian history

Forward trading in cotton and other goods existed in Indian markets for over a century, with long periods of bans. Modern exchange-traded equity derivatives began in June 2000, when index futures launched on the NSE and BSE. Index options followed in 2001, then stock options and stock futures the same year. National commodity exchanges like MCX and NCDEX opened in 2003, and currency futures in 2008. Commodity derivatives regulation moved to SEBI in 2015. Volumes grew enormously after 2020 as millions of individuals began trading options on their phones, which led to SEBI's studies and tighter rules from 2024.

Chapter 15

Rohit's rules

After watching Kamala's losses and learning to hedge, Rohit writes down his rules for derivatives. He keeps them on his phone's lock screen.

  • Hedge, don't bet. Use derivatives only to reduce a risk he already has, like protecting shares before an event.
  • Know the worst case in rupees before entering. If he can't say exactly how much he could lose, he doesn't trade.
  • Never sell options he can't cover. Covered calls on shares he owns are fine. Naked selling isn't.
  • Size matters more than direction. No position where a bad day could seriously hurt him.
  • Count all the costs: premium, brokerage, taxes, spread, and the time decay he is paying.
  • No borrowed money, ever.
  • Ignore anyone selling courses, tips or screenshots of profits. They are rarely showing the losses.

Most of the time, his best derivatives decision is none at all. His index fund and a few shares, held for years, don't need them.

Tax note

In India, profits and losses from futures and options are generally treated as non-speculative business income, taxed at your slab rate, with record-keeping and possibly audit requirements. Rules change, so check with a tax professional before trading.

The next harvest

Ten years on, Gopal leads a farmer producer organisation of 300 mango growers. Together they sell part of each year's crop forward to Sunrise and other buyers, and they buy price protection for the rest. A bad price year no longer means selling land. His son is studying agricultural economics.

Lakshmi's company knows its mango, oil and currency costs months in advance. Its profits are steadier, which, as the stock market story explained, makes investors willing to pay more for its shares.

Kamala has closed her trading account. She tells her story at the Kesarpur senior citizens' club, which she says is the best return she's ever made on ₹3.2 lakh.

That is what a derivative is: a contract whose value comes from something else's price, which lets people agree today on tomorrow's terms. And that is what the derivatives market is: a place where risk itself is bought and sold. In the hands of a farmer or a pickle maker, it is insurance. In the hands of someone chasing quick money with leverage, it is one of the fastest ways to lose it.

The four storiesBonds lend. Mutual funds pool. Shares own. Derivatives move risk. Together they make up most of what people mean by the financial markets.

Eight myths about derivatives

Derivatives are just gambling.They began as insurance for farmers and merchants, and most of their economic value still comes from hedging.
Buying options is low risk because loss is limited.Limited per trade, but most out-of-the-money options expire worthless. Repeated small losses add up to large ones.
Selling options is safe, steady income.Small regular gains, then rare but huge losses. Unlimited risk on naked calls.
A cheap option is a bargain.It's cheap because the market thinks it probably won't pay off.
Margin is the most you can lose.With futures and sold options, losses can exceed your deposit.
Hedging makes money.A hedge reduces risk and costs something. If your hedge often profits, it's probably a bet.
Notional value is money at risk.Notional is the size of the underlying. Actual exposure is usually much smaller, though it can grow fast.
A good strategy from a video will work for me.Screenshots show winners. SEBI's data shows that about nine in ten individual F&O traders lose.

Before any derivative trade: a checklist

  • I can name the specific risk this trade reduces, or I admit it's a bet.
  • I know the lot size and the full contract value I control.
  • I know my maximum loss in rupees, including an overnight gap.
  • I can meet margin calls without selling other things in a panic.
  • I understand how time decay and volatility affect this position.
  • I've counted premium, brokerage, taxes and the bid–ask spread.
  • I'm not using borrowed money or money I'll need soon.
  • I know what I'll do at expiry, including physical settlement for stock derivatives.
  • I've read the broker's risk disclosure.
  • I'm not acting on a tip, a course, or a screenshot of someone else's profits.

Glossary

American / European option
Exercisable any time before expiry / only at expiry.
Arbitrage
Profiting from the same thing priced differently in two places.
At / in / out of the money
Strike equal to / favourable to / unfavourable to the current price.
Basis
Gap between futures price and spot price.
Call option
The right to buy at the strike price.
Cash / physical settlement
Pay the price difference / actually deliver the underlying.
Clearing corporation
Guarantees trades by becoming the counterparty to both sides.
Cost of carry
Interest and storage costs that make futures differ from spot.
Covered call
Selling a call on shares you own.
Credit default swap
A contract that pays out if a borrower defaults.
Delta, gamma, theta, vega
Option sensitivity to price, delta's change, time, and volatility.
Expiry
The last day a contract exists.
Forward
Private agreement to trade later at a fixed price.
Future
Standardised, exchange-traded forward.
Hedging
Taking a position that offsets an existing risk.
Implied volatility
Expected price swings implied by option prices.
Intrinsic / time value
Value if exercised now / the extra paid for the chance of more.
Leverage
Controlling a large position with little money.
Lot size
The fixed quantity in one contract.
Margin
A deposit held against possible losses.
Margin call
A demand to add money after losses.
Mark-to-market
Daily settlement of gains and losses.
Notional
The face size of the underlying a contract covers.
Open interest
Number of contracts not yet closed.
Premium
The price paid for an option.
Protective put
Buying a put to insure shares you own.
Put option
The right to sell at the strike price.
Strike price
The fixed price in an option contract.
Swap
Exchange of one stream of payments for another.
Underlying
The asset or rate a derivative depends on.
