A story about the crypto market, in sixteen chapters

The Kesarpur Ledger

Varun is nineteen. His feed is full of people who turned ₹10,000 into ₹10 lakh with a coin he'd never heard of. His cousin Rohit has read eight stories about markets and is less sure. Follow them as they find out what crypto really is: an idea about a shared ledger, a new kind of asset with no earnings behind it, and a market full of both innovation and traps.

Varun, college student Rohit, his cousin Neha, analyst Mr. Iyer, bank Kamala, the great-aunt Salim, in Dubai

Read this first: crypto assets are highly volatile, mostly unregulated as investments in India, and a common route for scams. This page explains how they work. It does not recommend buying them.

Part one

How it works

Prologue

Ten thousand to ten lakh

Varun studies engineering at the district college. He's Rohit's younger cousin, and Kamala's other grandson. Every evening his phone shows him the same kind of video: a young man in a rented car, a screenshot of a crypto wallet up 9,000%, and a link to a Telegram group.

A classmate bought a "memecoin" with a dog on it and says it went up 40 times in three weeks. Varun has ₹25,000 saved from tutoring. He messages Rohit: "Should I put it all in?"

Rohit, who has learned a lot from his family's money stories, replies: "Before you buy anything, can you explain what it is?" Varun can't. So they decide to find out together, with help from Rohit's analyst friend Neha.

Ninth storyThis follows the bond, mutual fund, stock, derivatives, money market, forex, commodity and real estate stories. Kamala, Rohit, Iyer, Neha and Salim return.
SurvivorshipYou see the screenshots of people who won. You don't see the much larger number who lost and posted nothing.
Chapter 1

Money is a ledger

Neha starts with a question: when Rohit pays Varun ₹500 by UPI, what actually moves? No notes travel. Two banks change numbers in their records: Rohit's balance goes down by 500, Varun's goes up by 500. Most money today is simply entries in ledgers kept by banks, and those banks keep accounts at the RBI, which keeps the master ledger.

This works because we trust the record-keepers. Banks are regulated, audited and insured; the RBI stands behind them. The system stops one person from spending the same rupee twice, which is called the double-spending problem, because there's one official book.

But some people, especially after the 2008 financial crisis, asked: what if you didn't want to trust a bank or a government? Could strangers on the internet keep a shared ledger that nobody controls, and that nobody can cheat?

LedgerA record of who owns what and every transfer between them. Shopkeepers in Kesarpur still keep paper ones, called bahi-khata.
Double spendingPaying two people with the same money. Digital files can be copied, so digital money needs a way to stop this.
Chapter 2

A ledger with no owner

On 31 October 2008, in the middle of the financial crisis, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a nine-page paper describing "a peer-to-peer electronic cash system": Bitcoin. The network started in January 2009. Nobody knows for certain who Satoshi is.

The idea is simple to describe. Instead of one bank keeping the book, thousands of computers around the world each keep a full copy of the same ledger. When someone sends bitcoin, the transaction is broadcast to all of them. They check it against their copy: does the sender really have that much? Is it signed by the owner? If enough agree, it's added to everyone's book.

If one computer tries to change its copy, giving itself extra coins, everyone else's copies disagree, and it's ignored. Watch:

NodeA computer running the network's software and keeping a copy of the ledger.
DecentralisedNo single company, bank or government runs it. That's the core feature, and also why there's nobody to call when something goes wrong.
Chapter 3

Blocks, hashes and tampering

The ledger is written in pages called blocks. Each block holds a batch of transactions. Each block also contains a fingerprint of the block before it, so they form a chain: a blockchain.

The fingerprint is a hash: a short code calculated from all the data in the block. Change even one letter of the data, and the hash changes completely. Try it: type anything in the box and watch the hash.

This page uses a simple, fast hash for demonstration. Bitcoin uses SHA-256, which works on the same principle but is designed to be practically impossible to reverse or fake.

Now here's why the chain is hard to tamper with. Below are four blocks. Each stores the hash of the one before it. Press the button to secretly change a payment in Block 2.

Changing Block 2 changes its hash. Block 3 still holds the old hash of Block 2, so the link breaks, and so does every block after it. To cheat, you'd have to rebuild every later block, and do it faster than the rest of the network adds new ones. The next chapter shows why that's so expensive.

Chapter 4

Mining: who writes the next page?

If thousands of computers keep copies, who gets to add the next block? Bitcoin's answer is a competition called proof of work. Computers called miners race to find a number (a nonce) that, combined with the block's data, gives a hash starting with a required number of zeros. There's no shortcut; you can only guess, again and again.

The first miner to find one broadcasts the block. Others check it instantly (checking is easy; finding is hard), add it to their copy, and the winner receives newly created bitcoin plus transaction fees. Try mining a block yourself.

Guesses tried0
Nonce found—
Block data: "Varun pays Rohit 2 coins" · press Mine

Each extra zero makes the puzzle about 16 times harder here. The real network adjusts difficulty every two weeks so a new block appears roughly every 10 minutes, no matter how many miners join. Today, bitcoin miners worldwide make an almost unimaginable number of guesses every second, using warehouses of specialised machines.

That's what makes cheating so costly: to rewrite history, you'd need more computing power than everyone else combined. It's also why bitcoin uses a lot of electricity. Cambridge University estimates put its yearly use comparable to that of a mid-sized country.

Many newer networks use proof of stake instead. Validators lock up their own coins as a deposit and are chosen to add blocks; if they cheat, they lose their deposit. Ethereum switched to proof of stake in 2022, cutting its energy use by more than 99%.

HashA fixed-length fingerprint of data. Easy to compute, practically impossible to reverse, and changes completely with any edit.
Block rewardNew coins paid to the miner who adds a block. This is the only way new bitcoin is created.
51% attackIf one group controlled most of a network's mining or staking power, it could rewrite recent history. Small coins with few miners are most at risk.
Chapter 5

Only 21 million

Bitcoin's rules fix its total supply at 21 million coins, ever. The block reward started at 50 bitcoin per block and is cut in half about every four years, an event called the halving: 25 in 2012, 12.5 in 2016, 6.25 in 2020, 3.125 in 2024. The last fraction of a coin will be mined around 2140.

Supporters call bitcoin "digital gold": scarce, like gold in the commodity story, and impossible for any central bank to print more of. Critics answer that a fixed supply doesn't create value by itself: plenty of scarce things are worthless if nobody wants them. And unlike gold, bitcoin has no use in jewellery or industry. Its value depends entirely on people continuing to want it.

Many bitcoins are also gone for good. Researchers estimate that millions of coins sit in wallets whose owners have lost their keys.

SatoshiThe smallest unit of bitcoin: one hundred-millionth of a coin. You don't need to buy a whole bitcoin.
Other coinsNot every crypto has a cap. Some have unlimited or flexible supply, and many give large shares to their founders.
Chapter 6

Keys and wallets

Who owns which coins? There are no names on the blockchain, only addresses. Ownership comes from a pair of mathematically linked keys:

  • A private key: a secret number. Whoever knows it can spend the coins. It's like the key to a locker, the signature and the PIN all in one.
  • A public key and address: derived from the private key, and safe to share, like an account number.

A wallet is the software or device that stores your keys. Most wallets show a seed phrase of 12 or 24 words, which can regenerate all the keys. Lose it, and the coins are gone forever. Let someone see it, and they can take everything, with no bank to reverse it.

There are two ways to hold crypto:

  • Custodial: an exchange holds the keys for you, like a bank holds your money. Convenient, but if the exchange fails or is hacked, you may lose your coins (Chapter 11).
  • Self-custody: you hold your own keys, on an app or a hardware device. Nobody can freeze your coins, but nobody can help you if you make a mistake. Send to a wrong address, and it's gone.
Never shareNo genuine exchange, support agent or wallet will ever ask for your seed phrase or private key. Anyone who asks is stealing.
PseudonymousAddresses aren't names, but every transaction is public forever. Investigators routinely trace funds through the chain.
Chapter 7

Beyond bitcoin

Bitcoin was the first. Today there are thousands of crypto tokens, and the idea has spread far beyond digital cash. Neha sorts them into groups.

Bitcoin

The original. Designed as money and a store of value; deliberately simple and slow to change.

Ethereum and smart contracts

Launched in 2015. Its blockchain runs programs called smart contracts: code that moves tokens automatically when conditions are met. This made possible most of what follows.

DeFi

"Decentralised finance": lending, borrowing and trading run by smart contracts instead of banks. Innovative, but bugs and hacks have drained billions.

Stablecoins

Tokens designed to stay at a fixed value, usually $1. Widely used for trading and cross-border payments (Chapter 8).

NFTs

Non-fungible tokens: unique tokens recording ownership of a digital item. A digital artwork sold for $69 million in 2021; most NFTs from that boom later lost almost all their value.

Memecoins and new tokens

Coins based on jokes and internet trends, or freshly launched tokens. Anyone can create one in minutes. Many are pure speculation, and a large share collapse to zero or are scams (Chapter 12).

Varun's dog coin is a memecoin. It has no business, no earnings, and no purpose beyond being traded. Its creators kept a large slice of the supply at launch, and can sell whenever they like.

Chapter 8

Stablecoins and the e-rupee

Salim, who sends money home from Dubai in the forex story, hears that some workers use dollar stablecoins to send money. A stablecoin is meant to stay worth exactly $1, usually because its issuer holds dollars and short-term US government bonds as backing. The biggest ones are used heavily on crypto exchanges.

But "meant to" is the key phrase. Some stablecoins are backed by real reserves; others relied on algorithms. In May 2022, TerraUSD, an algorithmic stablecoin, lost its link to the dollar. Within days it fell to a few cents, and its sister coin, Luna, collapsed to almost nothing, wiping out around $40 billion.

For Salim, there's also the law: sending money to India through crypto isn't an authorised remittance channel under India's foreign exchange rules. He sticks with his remittance app.

The e-rupee is not crypto

Iyer explains that the RBI has its own digital currency, the e-rupee, piloted since late 2022. It's a central bank digital currency (CBDC): a digital form of the rupee note, issued and backed by the RBI, with the same value as cash. It isn't decentralised, it doesn't change in value against the rupee, and it isn't an investment.

PegA promise to keep a fixed value, like the dirham's peg in the forex story. Pegs are only as strong as the reserves and trust behind them.
Part two

The market

Chapter 9

What sets a coin's price?

Rohit asks Neha the question he'd ask about any investment: "What's it worth?" In every earlier story, there was an answer rooted in cash:

Most crypto produces no cash flow. So its price is set entirely by supply and demand: how many people want to hold it, and why. The forces that move it:

  • Belief and adoption: more users, payments or institutional buyers, such as the US bitcoin ETFs approved in 2024, raise demand.
  • Narratives: "digital gold", "the future of finance", or a celebrity post can move prices more than any fact.
  • Interest rates and liquidity: crypto has tended to boom when money is cheap and slump when central banks raise rates.
  • Leverage: many traders borrow heavily. When prices fall, forced selling of borrowed positions (liquidations) can crash prices in minutes.
  • Supply: halvings, token unlocks for founders, and new coins.
  • Regulation and hacks: bans, approvals and exchange failures cause sharp moves.

Neha's summary: "With a share, you can be wrong about the price but still own part of a real business. With most coins, if people stop wanting them, there's nothing underneath."

Greater foolAn investment whose only source of profit is selling to someone who'll pay more. It can work for a long time, until the buyers run out.
Market capPrice × coins in circulation. A tiny coin can show a huge market cap if a few coins trade at a high price while most of the supply is held by insiders.
Chapter 10

The wildest ride in finance

Here is bitcoin's price since 2013. It's on a logarithmic scale, so each gridline is ten times the one below. Even so, the swings are enormous.

Each of bitcoin's three big booms ended in a fall of roughly 75–85%. Anyone who bought at a peak waited years to break even. And bitcoin is the least volatile major coin; smaller coins routinely fall 90–99% and never recover.

DrawdownA fall from peak. From −80%, you need +400% just to get back to where you started.
CorrelationCrypto is often described as separate from stock markets, but in recent years it has often fallen together with shares in market sell-offs.
Chapter 11

When exchanges fail

Most people buy crypto through an exchange: an app or website that takes rupees and gives coins, holding the coins for you. That makes the exchange a kind of bank, but often without a bank's regulation, capital rules, audits or deposit insurance.

Mt. Gox, 2014Then the world's largest bitcoin exchange, based in Tokyo, collapsed after about 850,000 bitcoins disappeared through hacks and mismanagement. Customers waited about a decade for partial repayment.
FTX, 2022One of the biggest exchanges, valued at about $32 billion, collapsed within days in November 2022 after it emerged that customer money had been moved to its sister trading firm. Its founder was convicted of fraud and sentenced to 25 years in prison.
Lending platforms, 2022Platforms like Celsius that promised high "interest" on deposited crypto went bankrupt in the same crash, freezing customers' coins.
WazirX hack, 2024In July 2024, hackers stole roughly $230 million from one of India's largest crypto exchanges, which froze withdrawals for many users.
Proof of reservesSome exchanges now publish evidence that they hold customers' coins. It helps, but it doesn't always show debts, and isn't a full audit.
"Not your keys, not your coins"A common saying in crypto: if someone else holds the keys, you hold a promise, not the coins.
Chapter 12

Kamala and the trading bot

Kamala, who has lost money on options and silver in earlier stories, receives a friendly message from a wrong number. A polite young woman apologises, and they start chatting. Weeks later, the woman mentions her uncle's "AI crypto trading bot" that makes 3% a day. She sends screenshots of profits and a link to a smart-looking app.

Kamala deposits ₹20,000 through a UPI ID she's given. The app shows her balance growing every day. She deposits ₹1 lakh more. When she tries to withdraw, the app says she must first pay a 20% "tax" and "unlock fee". She pays. More fees appear. The woman stops replying.

The app was fake. The "profits" were numbers on a screen. This type of fraud, which builds trust slowly before the theft, is sometimes called a pig-butchering scam, and it has taken huge sums from people in India and worldwide.

Rohit helps Kamala report it immediately on India's cybercrime helpline, 1930, and at cybercrime.gov.in. Reporting fast can sometimes freeze money still in the receiving bank accounts. She recovers part of the first transfer.

Other common crypto scams: fake exchange apps; "guaranteed return" schemes that pay early investors with later investors' money; rug pulls, where creators of a new token sell all their coins and vanish; pump-and-dumps organised in Telegram groups; fake support agents who ask for your seed phrase; and impersonation of celebrities "giving away" crypto.

Red flagsGuaranteed or daily returns. Strangers who become friends online and then talk about investing. Payments to personal UPI IDs or bank accounts. Fees to withdraw your own money. Pressure to act fast.
HelpCall 1930 or report at cybercrime.gov.in. Also tell your bank immediately.
Chapter 13

Crypto in India: rules and tax

India's approach has changed several times:

  • 2018: the RBI told banks not to deal with crypto businesses.
  • 2020: the Supreme Court set that circular aside, and trading revived.
  • 2022: the government brought in a special tax regime for "virtual digital assets" (VDAs), including crypto and NFTs.
  • 2023: crypto platforms serving Indians were brought under anti-money-laundering law and must register with the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND). Later that year, the FIU acted against several offshore exchanges that hadn't registered.

Crypto is not legal tender in India, and there's no comprehensive law regulating it as an investment the way SEBI regulates shares and funds. The RBI has repeatedly warned about its risks.

How crypto gains are taxed

The VDA tax rules, as introduced in 2022, are much harsher than for shares:

  • Gains are taxed at a flat 30% (plus cess), whatever your income and however long you held.
  • No deduction except the purchase cost: no fees or other expenses.
  • Losses can't be set off against gains on other coins or any other income, and can't be carried forward.
  • A 1% TDS is deducted on sales above a threshold, which can be adjusted against your final tax.

Try what that means when you win on one coin and lose on another:

Your real net result+₹10,000

After tax−₹5,600

Rules changeCrypto rules and tax in India have changed repeatedly and may change again. This describes the structure introduced in 2022. Check current rules before acting.
RecordsEvery trade, including coin-to-coin swaps, can be a taxable transfer. Keep full records.
Chapter 14

The crypto market at scale

Zoom out from Kesarpur. The crypto market runs 24 hours a day, every day, across thousands of tokens and hundreds of exchanges worldwide. Its total value has swung by trillions of dollars between booms and busts.

Builders and networks

Open-source developers, foundations and companies building blockchains and applications. Some do serious engineering; others mainly sell tokens.

Miners and validators

Those who secure networks and add blocks, earning new coins and fees. Bitcoin mining is now dominated by large industrial operations.

Exchanges and custodians

Centralised exchanges, decentralised exchanges run by smart contracts, and custodians holding coins for institutions.

Investors and traders

Individuals, hedge funds, and since 2024 large asset managers through US bitcoin ETFs. Much of the trading volume is leveraged derivatives.

Regulators

Approaches differ: the US regulates through securities and commodities agencies; the EU introduced a dedicated crypto law, MiCA; El Salvador made bitcoin legal tender in 2021, then scaled that back in 2025; India taxes heavily and applies anti-money-laundering rules.

Criminals

Ransomware, scams, sanctions evasion and money laundering use crypto, though blockchain analysis firms help police trace many of these flows.

Underneath the speculation, some ideas from crypto are being taken seriously: tokenising bonds and funds, faster cross-border settlement, programmable payments. Central banks, including the RBI, are experimenting with similar technology in their own digital currencies. The technology and the speculation are two separate questions.

Chapter 15

If you still want to buy

Rohit's own view, after all eight earlier stories: he doesn't need crypto to reach his goals. His index funds, debt funds and a little gold do that. But he respects that some adults will decide to take a small, deliberate gamble. If so, these are the rules he'd insist on:

  • Only money you can lose completely without changing your life. For most people, a very small share of savings, if any. Never borrowed money, never your emergency fund.
  • Goals first. Emergency fund, insurance and long-term investments come before any crypto.
  • Use platforms registered with FIU-IND in India. Avoid apps and websites you find through ads, messages or social media.
  • Stick to the largest, longest-running coins if at all. Avoid memecoins, new tokens and anything promising returns.
  • No leverage, no futures, no lending your coins for "interest".
  • Protect access: strong passwords, app-based two-factor authentication, and if you self-custody, keep the seed phrase offline and private.
  • Keep tax records of every trade from day one.
  • Expect an 80% fall at some point, and decide in advance that you won't panic or add more to "win it back".
One simple testIf you can't explain what you're buying and why it has value, in two sentences, to Meera, don't buy it.
Chapter 16

Varun's decision

Three weeks later, Varun's classmate's dog coin has fallen 92% from its peak. Its creators had sold their tokens near the top. Varun is very glad he waited.

He puts ₹20,000 of his tutoring money into an index fund SIP, the way his cousin Ananya started. He keeps ₹5,000 as a deliberate, small experiment: he buys a little bitcoin on a registered Indian platform, writes down every trade for tax, and treats it as tuition. Mostly, though, he spends his evenings learning to write smart contracts, because the engineering fascinates him far more than the price.

Kamala tells her scam story at the senior citizens' club, right after her options story. She now forwards every "guaranteed returns" message to Rohit before replying.

That is what crypto is: a clever way to keep a shared ledger without a central owner, with tokens whose value depends on people continuing to believe in them. And that is what the crypto market is: a global, round-the-clock market where serious technology, wild speculation and outright fraud trade side by side, with far fewer of the protections that guard every other market in these stories.

After the story

Eight myths about crypto

Blockchain makes crypto safe.The chain is hard to tamper with. Exchanges, apps, smart contracts and your own phone are not.
It's anonymous.Every transaction is public forever; investigators trace them routinely.
Scarcity guarantees value.Scarce things are only valuable if people keep wanting them.
Stablecoins can't lose value.TerraUSD fell from $1 to cents in a week.
Big exchanges are safe like banks.FTX and Mt. Gox were among the biggest. Exchanges aren't insured like bank deposits.
Losses reduce my crypto tax.Under India's VDA rules, losses can't be set off against gains.
The e-rupee is a cryptocurrency.It's a digital rupee issued by the RBI, worth exactly ₹1.
AI trading bots earn guaranteed daily returns.Guaranteed returns are the clearest sign of a scam.

Before touching crypto: a checklist

  • My emergency fund, insurance and long-term investments are in place.
  • I can lose this whole amount without real harm.
  • I can explain in two sentences what I'm buying and why it might have value.
  • The platform is registered with FIU-IND and I found it myself, not through a message or ad.
  • I'm not using leverage, borrowed money, futures or lending products.
  • No one has promised me guaranteed or daily returns.
  • I'll never share my seed phrase, private key or OTP with anyone.
  • I understand the 30% tax, the 1% TDS, and that losses can't be set off.
  • I'm ready to see it fall 80% without panic-selling or doubling down.
