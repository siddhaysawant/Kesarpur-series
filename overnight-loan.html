A story about the money market, in sixteen chapters

The Kesarpur Overnight Loan

At 4 pm on a Friday, the Kesarpur Cooperative Bank is ₹15 crore short. By 5 pm it has borrowed the money from a bank in Mumbai, for one weekend. Follow that loan, and the loans around it, and you will understand the money market: the hidden place where banks, companies and governments lend each other money for a day, a week or a few months.

Part one

Borrowing until tomorrow

Prologue

Four o'clock on a Friday

Mr. Iyer has run the treasury at the Kesarpur Cooperative Bank for eleven years. It's the same bank that bought Meera's bridge bond in the first story. His job is not to choose who gets loans. His job is to make sure that, at every moment, the bank has enough cash.

This Friday is difficult. It's the last day of the month, so the whole town is withdrawing salaries. It's also an advance-tax date, and the bank's business customers have sent ₹18 crore to the government. A large loan to a rice mill went out at 2:30. By 4 pm, the bank's account at the central bank has fallen below the minimum it is legally required to keep there.

The bank isn't in trouble. It has ₹1,200 crore of loans and investments. But nearly all of that money is tied up: in home loans repayable over twenty years, in government bonds, in the rice mill's new machines. None of it can become cash by 5 pm.

Iyer needs ₹15 crore, until Monday. Somewhere in India, another bank has the opposite problem: ₹15 crore it doesn't need until Monday, sitting idle and earning nothing. The place where those two banks find each other is the money market.

Fifth storyThis follows the bond, mutual fund, stock market and derivatives stories. The cooperative bank, Farida, Lakshmi and Ananya all appear again.
LiquidityHaving cash available when payments are due. A bank can be solvent (owning more than it owes) and still be illiquid (short of cash today).
Chapter 1

Money that sleeps, money that's short

Every organisation has the same daily problem as Iyer, just on a different scale. Money comes in and goes out at different times. A company collects payments at month-end but pays wages on the 1st. A town collects property tax in April but spends all year. The government collects most of its tax in a few months and spends every day.

So at any moment, some have money that sleeps: cash they won't need for a day, a week or three months. Others have money that's short: a temporary gap. The money market is where they lend to each other for short periods, from one day up to one year.

It's different from the markets in the earlier stories in four ways:

  • Short. Overnight to one year. Bonds and shares are for years or forever.
  • Large. Deals are usually in crores. It's a wholesale market for banks, companies, funds and governments. Individuals mostly reach it through funds.
  • Safe, mostly. Lending for a day to a sound bank or the government carries very little risk. Prices barely move, because there's so little time for anything to go wrong.
  • Invisible. No ticker on the news, no bell ringing. But if it stops working, ATMs, salaries and payments stop too.
Money marketThe market for borrowing and lending for up to one year. Its rates are the shortest-term "price of money" in the economy.
The plumbingBankers often call the money market the financial system's plumbing. Nobody notices it until it stops working.
Chapter 2

The call money market

At 4:05 pm, Iyer logs in to the central bank's dealing screen. It shows banks offering to lend overnight and the rates they're asking. A large private bank in Mumbai offers ₹50 crore at 6.10%. Iyer takes ₹15 crore.

This is a call money loan: an overnight loan from one bank to another, with no security. The Mumbai bank trusts that a regulated bank will repay tomorrow. Because it's Friday, "overnight" means until the next working day, Monday, so the loan runs for three days.

Interest = Amount × Rate × Days ÷ 365 = ₹15,00,00,000 × 6.10% × 3 ÷ 365 ≈ ₹75,205

On Monday morning, Iyer's bank repays ₹15 crore plus ₹75,205. For the Mumbai bank, idle money earned ₹75,000 over a weekend. For Iyer, ₹75,000 was a small price for staying within the law and keeping every ATM working.

Hundreds of banks do this every afternoon. The average rate on all those overnight loans, weighted by size, is the weighted average call rate (WACR). It's one of the most important numbers in the economy, because it's the rate the central bank is trying to steer (Chapter 4).

Loans of 2 to 14 days are called notice money, and 15 days to a year, term money. Only banks and a few specified institutions can borrow and lend in this unsecured interbank market, and each bank has limits on how much it can borrow.

Why "call"The money is lent "at call": it is repayable almost immediately. It's the shortest loan there is.
MIBORThe Mumbai Interbank Offered Rate, a published benchmark for overnight interbank lending. Many swaps and floating-rate products are priced off overnight rates like it.
UnsecuredIf the borrowing bank failed overnight, the lender would be an ordinary creditor. That's why rates jump for banks that others start to doubt.
Chapter 3

Repo: a loan against a pledge

The next month, call money is expensive and Iyer's limit is used up. He has another option. The bank owns government bonds. He can pledge them to get a loan.

It works like a gold loan. Kesarpur families pledge jewellery to a lender, get cash, and pay a bit extra to get the jewellery back. In a repo (short for repurchase agreement), the bank sells government securities to a lender today and agrees to buy them back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. The difference is the interest.

Because the lender holds safe collateral, repo rates are usually a little lower than unsecured call rates, and lenders who aren't banks, like mutual funds and insurers, are happy to take part.

In India, most of this happens on TREPS (tri-party repo), a system run by the Clearing Corporation of India (CCIL). CCIL sits in the middle like the clearing house in the derivatives story: it holds the collateral, guarantees both sides, and settles the cash. Hundreds of participants trade in it every day without needing to trust each other directly.

From the lender's side, the same deal is a reverse repo: buying securities today with an agreement to sell them back.

RepoSell securities now, buy them back later at a fixed higher price. In effect, a secured loan.
HaircutLending less than the collateral's value. Riskier collateral gets a bigger haircut.
CollateralMostly government securities and T-bills. That's one reason banks hold so many government bonds: they double as a key to emergency cash.
Chapter 4

The central bank's window

What if every bank is short at once, and no one has spare money to lend? Then there's one lender that can never run out of rupees: the central bank, which creates them. In India, that's the Reserve Bank of India (RBI).

The RBI runs a set of facilities called the liquidity adjustment facility (LAF). Together they form a corridor that keeps overnight rates in a narrow band.

  • The policy repo rate is the rate at which the RBI lends to banks against government securities, through repo auctions. This is the "repo rate" in the news when the RBI changes rates.
  • The marginal standing facility (MSF) is the ceiling: a bank can always borrow overnight at repo + 0.25%, even using some of the securities it's required to hold. It's the emergency window, and expensive on purpose.
  • The standing deposit facility (SDF) is the floor: a bank with spare cash can always park it with the RBI at repo − 0.25%, with no collateral needed.

No bank will lend to another for less than the SDF, because it can earn that risk-free from the RBI. No bank will borrow for more than the MSF, because the RBI will lend at that rate. So the overnight rate stays inside the corridor. Where it sits inside depends on how much spare cash the system has. Try it below. (Rates are illustrative.)

Lender of last resortThe central bank's role of lending to sound banks when no one else will, to stop a cash shortage turning into a collapse.
Operating targetThe RBI's monetary policy aims to keep the weighted average call rate close to the policy repo rate. Everything else follows from there.
Variable rate auctionsBesides the standing windows, the RBI holds auctions to add or absorb money for days or weeks when the system has a large shortage or surplus.
Chapter 5

Why the whole system runs short

Iyer's Friday wasn't just bad luck. Some days, the whole banking system is short of cash at once, for reasons no single bank controls. Money market dealers keep calendars of these days.

Tax days

On advance tax dates (mid-June, September, December and March) and GST due dates, lakhs of crores move from company bank accounts to the government's account at the RBI. That money leaves the banking system until the government spends it.

Festivals and cash

Before Diwali or weddings, people withdraw notes. Cash in wallets is cash no longer in banks.

Defending the rupee

When the RBI sells dollars to stop the rupee falling, banks pay it rupees, which drains liquidity. Buying dollars does the reverse.

Government spending

When the government pays salaries, pensions and contractors, money flows back into banks and liquidity improves.

Reserve rules

Banks must keep a share of deposits as cash with the RBI, the cash reserve ratio (CRR), and a larger share in safe assets like government securities, the statutory liquidity ratio (SLR). Changing CRR is a powerful way for the RBI to add or remove money.

Month and quarter ends

Salaries, balance-sheet reporting and bunched payments make month-ends tighter, and banks often hold more cash than usual on those days.

Because these swings are predictable, the RBI watches them and runs auctions to add money before tax days and absorb it after big government spending. Its aim is to keep the overnight rate near the policy rate, so that day-to-day plumbing doesn't change the cost of money for everyone else.

Part two

The instruments

Chapter 6

Treasury bills

Farida, the Kesarpur town treasurer, has the opposite problem from Iyer. In April, the town collects most of its property tax. She has ₹20 crore she won't need until July. Leaving it in a current account earns nothing. Lending it to a company is too risky for public money. She needs somewhere completely safe for three months.

She buys Treasury bills, or T-bills: short-term loans to the national government. The government sells them every week through auctions run by the RBI, in three lengths: 91 days, 182 days and 364 days.

T-bills pay no interest coupon. They're sold at a discount and repaid at face value, like the zero-coupon bond in the bond story. At this week's auction, the cut-off price for 91-day bills is ₹98.50 for every ₹100 of face value.

Yield = (Face − Price) ÷ Price × 365 ÷ Days = (100 − 98.50) ÷ 98.50 × 365 ÷ 91 ≈ 6.11% a year

Farida pays ₹19.70 crore for ₹20 crore of bills. In July, the government pays her ₹20 crore. The town earns ₹30 lakh, with essentially no risk of default, and she can sell the bills any day if she needs the money early.

Chapter 7

Sunrise's commercial paper

Every April and May, Lakshmi's company, Sunrise Foods, has to buy most of a year's mangoes in eight weeks. It then sells the pickle slowly over the next twelve months. That leaves a gap of about ₹50 crore for roughly three months.

Her bank offers a working-capital loan at 9.5%. Her finance head suggests something cheaper. Sunrise is now a large, profitable, listed company with an A1+ short-term rating, the highest. It can borrow directly from investors by issuing commercial paper (CP): an unsecured short-term IOU, sold at a discount, like a T-bill but from a company.

Who buys the CP? Mostly liquid mutual funds (like Ananya's, Chapter 9), and banks and companies with spare cash. They earn more than on T-bills because Sunrise is riskier than the government.

There's one important danger. Many companies don't repay CP from profits; they repay maturing CP by issuing new CP, called rolling over. That works as long as investors keep buying. If they suddenly stop, as happened to many Indian finance companies in 2018 (Chapter 12), a perfectly profitable company can be unable to repay on the day. That's why companies issuing CP are expected to keep bank credit lines in reserve.

Commercial paperAn unsecured, short-term promissory note issued by a company, usually for 7 days to a year, at a discount.
Short-term ratingsAgencies rate CP on a separate scale: A1+ is the highest, then A1, A2, A3, A4, and D for default.
Rollover riskThe danger that you can't refinance a maturing loan. The shorter your borrowing, the more often you face it.
Chapter 8

Certificates of deposit

Banks raise short-term money too. When Iyer's bank wants to lend more in harvest season than its ordinary deposits allow, it can issue a certificate of deposit (CD): a large fixed deposit in tradable form.

A normal fixed deposit is a private contract between you and your bank. If you need the money early, you break it and pay a penalty. A CD is issued in large amounts, at a discount, and is tradable: a mutual fund that bought a 6-month CD can sell it to another investor next week, without the bank being involved.

CDA tradable, discounted deposit certificate issued by a bank, usually in large amounts to institutions.
Bank fundingWhen banks issue many CDs at rising rates, it often signals that loans are growing faster than deposits, a useful clue about the economy.
Chapter 9

Ananya's liquid fund

Ananya, the nurse from the mutual fund story, keeps her emergency money (six months of expenses) in a liquid fund. After the debt fund scare in that story, she wants to know exactly what it holds. She opens the monthly portfolio.

Her liquid fund is a pot of exactly the instruments in this story: lending to the government, to banks, and to strong companies like Sunrise, for a few weeks at a time. Its NAV rises a little nearly every day, as the discounts on those instruments shrink toward face value.

Rules protect her. SEBI requires liquid funds to hold only instruments maturing within 91 days, to keep a set minimum in cash, T-bills and government repo so they can always pay redemptions, and to limit how much they put into any one sector. Redemptions are usually paid the next working day, and many funds offer instant redemption of a small amount, up to ₹50,000, to a bank account.

An overnight fund goes further: it only lends for one day, mostly through TREPS. It earns a little less but has almost no credit or interest-rate risk. Ananya keeps one month's expenses there and the rest in her liquid fund.

Money market fundsThe general name for funds that hold short-term instruments. In India: overnight, liquid and money market fund categories.
Not a bank depositLiquid funds are not insured and can, rarely, lose value if a holding defaults. Check that the CP and CDs held are high quality.
Exit loadLiquid funds charge a small exit load if you redeem within 7 days, to discourage very short in-and-out trading.
Chapter 10

The ladder of short rates

Iyer keeps a sheet on his desk showing today's rates across the money market. Every number on it is linked to every other, and all of them hang from the policy rate.

Each step on the ladder is a spread: the extra pay for some extra risk. Secured is cheaper than unsecured. Government is cheaper than banks, and banks cheaper than companies. Three months costs a little more than overnight. When markets are calm, the spreads are small. When fear rises, the bottom of the ladder barely moves while the top shoots up, because everyone wants to lend only to the safest borrowers. Watching those spreads is how Iyer senses trouble early.

Short end of the curveThese rates form the first part of the yield curve from the bond story. The money market sets the short end; the bond market sets the long end.
Part three

The market and the economy

Chapter 11

From the central bank to your loan

Inflation is running high. The RBI's Monetary Policy Committee raises the policy repo rate by 0.25%. Here is how that decision travels through the money market to Kesarpur's kitchens.

This chain is called monetary policy transmission, and the money market is its first and most important link. If the money market didn't follow the policy rate, the RBI's decisions would have no effect. That's why the RBI spends so much effort keeping overnight rates inside the corridor: a central bank that can't control the overnight rate can't control anything else.

Policy stanceBesides changing the rate, the RBI signals its intentions and manages how much liquidity is in the system. A system short of cash pushes rates toward the ceiling, which tightens policy even without a rate change.
Link to the bond storyIn the bond story, the "central bank raised rates" and Meera's bond price fell. This chapter shows how that rate rise starts.
Chapter 12

When the plumbing froze

Money markets rarely make the news. When they do, it's usually because they've suddenly stopped working. Each time, the pattern is the same: lenders lose confidence, refuse to roll over short-term loans, and borrowers that looked healthy can't pay what's due tomorrow.

2008: the fund that broke the buckWhen Lehman Brothers collapsed in September 2008, the Reserve Primary Fund, a large US money market fund, held Lehman's commercial paper. Its value per unit fell below $1, which money market funds had almost never allowed. Panicked investors pulled hundreds of billions from similar funds within days, and companies that depended on CP suddenly couldn't borrow. The US government guaranteed money market funds to stop the run. In India, the RBI cut reserve ratios and opened special lending windows, including support for mutual funds facing redemptions.
2018: IL&FSWhen the infrastructure lender IL&FS defaulted (it appears in the bond and fund stories), investors stopped buying CP from non-bank finance companies. Many of these firms had funded long-term loans with short-term CP. With rollovers refused, several were squeezed hard and some failed. The lesson: borrowing short to lend long only works while the short-term market stays open.
2019: the US repo spikeIn September 2019, overnight repo rates in the US briefly jumped to around 10% because cash was unexpectedly scarce on a tax and settlement day. The US Federal Reserve had to inject money directly. Even the safest secured market can seize up when cash is short.
March 2020: the dash for cashAs Covid spread, investors everywhere tried to sell almost everything to hold cash, even safe short-term instruments. Central banks, including the RBI, provided large amounts of liquidity through long-term repo operations and other windows.

The common thread is maturity mismatch: borrowing for a day or a month to fund things that last years. Banks, finance companies and some funds all do it, because short-term money is cheaper. It's safe in calm times and dangerous in a panic. That's why regulators now require banks to hold enough high-quality liquid assets to survive a 30-day stress (the liquidity coverage ratio), and why central banks act fast when the plumbing freezes.

RunWhen many lenders or depositors try to get their money out at once, because they fear others will get there first. Runs can topple institutions that would otherwise have survived.
Breaking the buckWhen a money market fund's unit value falls below its stable price. It shows that "cash-like" is not the same as cash.
Chapter 13

The money market at scale

Zoom out from Kesarpur. On a typical day, money market deals across India's call, repo and TREPS markets run to several lakh crore rupees, nearly all settled the same day, mostly unseen by anyone outside a few hundred treasury desks.

The Reserve Bank of India

Sets the policy rate, runs the corridor and auctions, sets CRR and SLR, issues T-bills for the government, and regulates most money market instruments.

Banks

The core participants: borrowing and lending every day to meet reserve rules and customer payments. Large banks are often net lenders; smaller and fast-growing ones are often net borrowers.

Primary dealers

Firms authorised to bid in government securities auctions and keep the market active, borrowing in call and repo markets to fund their holdings.

Mutual funds and insurers

Huge lenders through TREPS, T-bills, CP and CDs. Liquid funds are one of the biggest buyers of CP in India.

Companies and governments

Companies borrow through CP and park surplus cash in liquid funds. State governments, towns like Kesarpur and public bodies manage cash through T-bills and deposits.

The infrastructure

CCIL clears and guarantees repo and TREPS trades. The RBI's NDS-Call and NDS-OM platforms host dealing. FIMMDA, the industry body for fixed income and money markets, sets market practices.

Chapter 14

The money market for you

Individuals don't trade call money, but the money market touches every household's savings. Here's where Kesarpur's families keep money they might need soon:

Meera, now in her eighties, keeps her monthly expenses in her savings account and three months' worth in a short FD. Ananya splits her emergency fund between an overnight fund and a liquid fund. Rohit uses a sweep-in account so his salary doesn't sit idle.

On tax: in India, interest from deposits and T-bills is added to your income and taxed at your slab rate. Gains from liquid and overnight funds bought after 1 April 2023 are also taxed at your slab rate, whenever you sell. Rules change, so check before comparing.

Return of money firstFor money you may need soon, safety and access matter far more than an extra half percent. Chasing yield with emergency money is how people end up in risky CP.
Rates move with policyWhen the RBI raises rates, overnight and liquid funds start earning more within days; FDs you already hold don't change.
Chapter 15

Mr. Iyer's rules

Years later, training his replacement, Iyer writes down what the money market taught him.

  • Liquidity before profit. A bank that is solvent but out of cash for one afternoon can still be destroyed. Always know where the next ₹15 crore will come from.
  • Keep the key to the window. Hold enough government securities to borrow in repo or from the RBI on the worst day.
  • Know the calendar. Tax days, month-ends, festivals and government payment days are predictable. Plan for them.
  • Don't fund long with short too heavily. The cheaper short-term money is, the more tempting the mismatch, and the more painful the day it can't be rolled.
  • Never rely on one lender. Spread borrowing across banks, repo, TREPS and the RBI window.
  • Watch the spreads. When the gap between government and company rates suddenly widens, someone knows something.
  • Cash-like isn't cash. Every instrument other than central bank money has some risk, however small.
Asset-liability managementA bank's discipline of matching when money comes in with when it must go out. Iyer's treasury reports to an ALM committee every month.
Chapter 16

Monday morning

At 9:30 on Monday, government salary payments arrive, and the cooperative bank's account at the RBI climbs back to ₹38 crore. Iyer repays the Mumbai bank's ₹15 crore plus ₹75,205. Neither bank thinks about it again.

In the same hour, Farida's T-bills earn another day's interest. Sunrise's commercial paper funds another truckload of mangoes. Ananya's liquid fund NAV ticks up by a fraction of a paisa. The RBI publishes its liquidity numbers, and the overnight rate sits a few basis points from the policy rate, exactly where it should be.

None of this was on the news. That's the sign it worked.

That is the money market: a market for time measured in days, where cash that would otherwise sleep is lent to someone who is short, for a price set by the central bank's corridor and by trust. And that is why every other market in these stories, bonds, funds, shares and derivatives, depends on it working every single day.

After the story

Six myths about the money market

It's the market for money in general.It's specifically the market for short-term borrowing and lending, up to a year.
A bank short of cash is a failing bank.Sound banks borrow overnight all the time. It's how daily payments balance.
Liquid funds are the same as savings accounts.They're usually very safe, but not insured, and can lose value if a holding defaults.
The repo rate is just a headline number.It anchors overnight rates, which flow into FD rates, loan EMIs and company borrowing costs.
Short-term lending has no risk.The 2008 and 2018 episodes showed that rollover and credit risk can strike fast.
Higher yield on a liquid fund is free money.It usually means more company CP or lower-rated paper. Extra yield always comes from extra risk.

Before parking short-term money: a checklist

  • I know when I might need this money, to the month.
  • For emergencies, I can reach some of it the same day.
  • I've chosen safety and access before yield.
  • For a liquid fund, I've checked the portfolio: mostly T-bills, repo, strong-bank CDs and A1+ CP.
  • I know any exit load and redemption timing.
  • I've compared after-tax returns, not headline rates.
  • My deposits in any one bank are within the insured limit, or I accept the risk.
  • I understand that rates on floating options will fall when the RBI cuts.
