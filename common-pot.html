Part one

The pot is built

Prologue

Five thousand rupees and a problem

Ananya is the granddaughter whose nursing fees Meera paid by selling her bridge bond. She is now twenty-five and a staff nurse at the Kesarpur district hospital, on the far side of the bridge her town once borrowed to build. She earns ₹40,000 a month. After rent, food, a little sent home and a little spent on herself, about ₹5,000 is left over.

She knows that money in a savings account at 3% barely keeps up with prices. She has heard that over long periods company shares have grown much faster. But she has three problems.

  • Too little money to spread. A single share of a big bank costs well over ₹1,000. With ₹5,000 she could own pieces of maybe three companies. If one of them fails, she loses a third of her savings.
  • No time. She works twelve-hour shifts. She can't read annual reports, follow quarterly results or watch prices all day.
  • No training. She can read a patient's chart in seconds, but not a balance sheet.

Her great-aunt Kamala, who once bought cement shares instead of bonds, tells her to pick "good companies". Her grandmother Meera tells her to avoid anything she doesn't understand. Both are right, and neither tells her what to do.

Companion storyThis continues The Kesarpur Bridge Bond. You don't need to have read it, but bonds come back in Chapters 4, 12 and 15, because many mutual funds hold bonds.
The small saver's problemDiversification, expertise and time all cost more than a small saver has. Every mutual fund in the world exists to solve these three problems at once.
Chapter 1

The nurses' pot

In the staff canteen, Ananya mentions her problem. It turns out two hundred other nurses, ward boys and technicians have the same one. The head nurse, Sister Thomas, has an idea.

Sister Thomas"Alone, each of us can buy three companies. Together, two hundred of us with ₹5,000 each have ₹10 lakh. That's enough to own forty companies. We hire one person who knows what they're doing to choose them, and we share whatever happens, in proportion to what we each put in."

That is the whole idea of a mutual fund: many people pool their money, a professional invests it in a spread of shares, bonds or both, and each person owns a slice of the whole pot. Gains and losses are shared in exact proportion.

Then the doubts start. Who holds the money? What stops the person running it from spending it on themselves, or buying shares in a cousin's company? How do you know what your slice is worth? What if you need your money back and everyone else wants to stay? What if a hundred new people want to join next month at a different price?

Pots like this, run on trust between colleagues, have collapsed many times in history. Somebody ran off with the money, or the books were wrong, or the early members were paid with the latecomers' money. Sister Thomas's idea is right, but it needs rules, guards and a fair way to count. That is what the next three chapters are about.

DefinitionA mutual fund is a pool of money from many investors, managed by professionals and invested in securities such as shares and bonds. Each investor owns units of the pool.
Mutual means sharedEveryone in the pot shares the same returns. Nobody gets a guaranteed amount. That is the key difference from Meera's bond, where the return was a fixed promise.
DiversificationOwning many investments so no single one can do much damage. It reduces the risk of any one company failing, but it can't remove the risk of the whole market falling.
Chapter 2

Who guards the pot

Sister Thomas writes to a cousin who works in finance in Mumbai. He explains that a real mutual fund is built so that no single party ever has both the money and the power to move it unchecked. In India, the structure is laid down by the market regulator, SEBI. Each role exists to guard against a particular way the pot could go wrong.

RegulatorSEBIWrites the rules, registers every fund, sets limits on costs and risks, inspects, and punishes wrongdoing.
↓ registers and supervises everyone below
FounderSponsorThe company that sets up the fund, puts in its own capital, and must meet SEBI's tests for track record and net worth. Like the promoter of a company.
Owner on paperTrust & trusteesThe fund is legally a trust. Its assets belong to the trust for the benefit of unitholders, not to the sponsor or manager. Trustees, many of them independent, watch the manager for the investors.
The managerAsset management company (AMC)Hired by the trustees to run the money: research, buying, selling. Earns a fee. Can decide what to buy, but never holds the money itself.
Safe keepingCustodianA separate bank that actually holds the shares and bonds and settles trades. The AMC gives orders; the custodian carries them out.
Record keepingRegistrar (RTA)Keeps the list of every investor and how many units they own, and processes purchases and redemptions. In India, mostly CAMS and KFintech.
CheckingAuditorsIndependent accountants who check the fund's books every year, separately from the AMC's own accounts.
↓ invest and redeem through
YouInvestors (unitholders)Buy units directly from the AMC, or through a distributor, adviser, broker or app. Your units are held in your own name in the registrar's records, or in your demat account.

Here is why this matters. Suppose the AMC that manages Ananya's fund goes bankrupt. Her money is not part of the AMC's assets. It sits with the custodian, owned by the trust on behalf of unitholders. The trustees would simply hire a different AMC to manage it. The risk that remains is the one she signed up for: the investments themselves going up or down.

SEBI's rules add more guards. A fund can't put more than a set share of its money into a single company. It must publish its full portfolio regularly and its unit price every business day. Managers and their staff must report their own trades. And every advertisement must carry the line you have probably heard said very fast at the end of a radio ad: mutual fund investments are subject to market risks, read all scheme-related documents carefully.

SeparationThe one who decides (AMC), the one who holds (custodian), the one who records (registrar), and the one who watches (trustees) are separate. No single party can make your money disappear.
Scheme documentsEvery fund publishes a Scheme Information Document (SID) with its objective, strategy, risks and costs, and a short Key Information Memorandum (KIM). The "read all scheme-related documents" line refers to these.
KYCBefore investing you complete Know Your Customer checks once (PAN, address, photo). It's valid across all fund houses.
Chapter 3

Units and NAV

The pot needs a fair way to count. The answer is units. On the first day, the fund sells units at ₹10 each. Two hundred people put in ₹5,000 each, ₹10 lakh in total, and receive 500 units each. The fund has 1,00,000 units.

A month later, the shares in the pot have moved. Some went up, some down. Every evening, after the market closes, the fund adds up what everything it owns is worth, takes away what it owes, and divides by the number of units. That figure is the net asset value per unit, the NAV.

NAV = (Market value of everything owned − Everything owed) ÷ Units outstanding = 10,65,000 ÷ 1,00,000 = ₹10.65

Ananya's 500 units are now worth 500 × ₹10.65 = ₹5,325. Nobody had to work out her share separately. Units do it automatically.

Now a new nurse, Joseph, wants to join with ₹5,000. He doesn't get units at ₹10. He gets them at today's NAV: ₹5,000 ÷ 10.65 = 469.484 units. The fund takes his ₹5,000 in cash, issues new units, and buys more shares. Nobody already in the pot gains or loses from his joining. If Ananya leaves, the reverse happens: the fund pays her 500 × NAV, cancels her units, and sells shares if it needs the cash. This is an open-ended fund: units are created and cancelled every day at NAV.

NAVThe value of one unit. Published every business day. In an open-ended fund you always buy and sell at NAV (minus any exit load, Chapter 7).
Cut-off timeYour order gets the NAV of the day only if it reaches the fund, with the money, before the cut-off (3 pm for most funds on business days). Otherwise you get the next day's NAV. This stops anyone buying at yesterday's price after seeing today's news.
Open vs. closedAn open-ended fund issues and redeems units any day. A closed-ended fund sells units once, for a fixed term, and you can only exit early by selling on a stock exchange, often below NAV. An interval fund opens for redemptions at set intervals.

Chapter 4

The menu of funds

Ananya opens a fund app and finds more than a thousand schemes. It looks like chaos, but there is a simple order underneath. In 2017 SEBI made every fund house fit each scheme into a defined category with a clear rule, and allowed only one scheme per category per fund house, with a few exceptions. The first question is always: what does the pot hold?

Equity funds: mostly company shares

For goals at least five to seven years away. Highest long-run growth, largest falls. Size categories follow a fixed list: India's top 100 listed companies by market value are large caps, 101 to 250 are mid caps, the rest small caps.

The riskometer

Every scheme must display a riskometer, a dial with six levels, updated every month based on what the fund actually holds. Each fund is also compared with a benchmark: the index it is trying to beat, such as a top-100 index for a large-cap fund.

Chapter 5

A day with the fund manager

Ananya chooses a flexi cap fund run by a fund house in Pune. Its manager is Ravi. Ananya will never meet him, but here is roughly what his day looks like.

7:30 am. Ravi reads overnight news: US markets, oil prices, the rupee, and a cement company's results announced late last night.

8:30 am. Morning meeting with eight analysts, each covering an industry. The banking analyst thinks loan growth is slowing. The pharma analyst has visited a factory and is impressed. They argue. Ravi listens and asks what would prove each of them wrong.

9:15 am. The market opens. Ravi has decided to sell a quarter of the fund's holding in one bank and add to the pharma company. He does not press buttons himself. He gives the order to the fund's dealing desk, who split it into small pieces through the day so the fund's large order doesn't push the price against it.

11:00 am. A call with a company's management, along with thirty other investors. Ravi asks about debt, about the founder's plans to take money out, and about why a competitor is growing faster.

2:00 pm. A risk review. The compliance team checks that no single stock is above SEBI's limits, that the fund still fits its category rules, and that the portfolio's risk is within the fund's own limits. Cash flows matter too: this month, SIP money is arriving faster than redemptions, so Ravi has fresh cash to invest each day.

After 3:30 pm. The market closes. The fund's accountants value every holding at its closing price, and the NAV is published that evening.

Ravi's job comes down to three decisions: which companies to own (stock selection), how much of each (position sizing), and when to change his mind (discipline). His results are judged against the benchmark, after costs, over years, not days.

Investment processGood fund houses write down how they pick stocks: growth, value, quality, or a mix. Consistency matters: a manager who changes style every year is hard to judge and hard to trust.
Portfolio turnoverHow much of the fund is bought and sold each year. High turnover means more trading costs and often more short-term thinking.
Manager riskIf a star manager leaves, the fund you bought may behave differently. Factsheets name the manager and how long they've run the fund.
Chapter 6

Active or passive

Ananya's colleague Joseph took a different path. He bought an index fund, which has no Ravi. It simply holds the companies in a chosen index, such as the 50 largest, in the same proportions as the index, and changes only when the index changes.

Joseph's argument goes like this. The whole market is made up of all investors. Before costs, the average rupee invested must earn the market's return, because together they are the market. Active managers as a group charge more and trade more. So, after costs, the average actively managed rupee must earn less than the market. Some managers beat it, but it's hard to know in advance which ones, and few keep doing it for long.

The evidence supports Joseph more often than not. Regular studies comparing funds with their benchmarks, such as S&P's SPIVA reports, have found that over periods of five to ten years most actively managed large-cap funds in India and the US trail their index after costs. The picture is more mixed for mid and small caps, where markets are less thoroughly researched.

Ananya's argument for her flexi cap fund: a good manager can avoid obviously over-priced or badly run companies that an index must hold, and can move between large, mid and small companies. She is willing to pay more for the chance of doing better, and accepts that she might do worse.

IndexA list of companies chosen by fixed rules, used to measure a market. An index fund owns the list.
Tracking errorHow much an index fund's return differs from its index, due to costs, cash and timing. Lower is better.
HistoryThe first index fund for ordinary investors was launched in the US in 1976 by John Bogle at Vanguard. Critics called it "un-American". Today passive funds hold a large and fast-growing share of fund money worldwide, and in India.
Chapter 7

The price of the pot

Ravi, his analysts, the custodian, the registrar, the auditors and the trustees all have to be paid. That money comes out of the pot, a tiny slice every day. The yearly total, as a share of the fund's assets, is the total expense ratio (TER). It is already deducted from the NAV, so you never see a bill. That makes it easy to ignore.

Ananya meets Dev, a distributor at her bank branch. He is friendly and helpful and offers to set everything up. What he doesn't stress is that every fund has two versions:

  • A regular plan, bought through a distributor like Dev. Part of the expense ratio is paid to the distributor as commission, every year, for as long as you hold.
  • A direct plan, bought straight from the fund house or a direct platform. Same fund, same manager, same holdings, but no commission, so a lower expense ratio and a higher NAV over time.

The difference is often around 0.5% to 1% a year. That sounds tiny. Here is what it does to a ₹5,000 monthly SIP, if the fund earns 13% a year before costs.

Dev's advice still has value for some people. A good distributor or adviser can stop you making expensive mistakes, such as panic-selling in a crash, which can cost far more than 1%. The point is to know what you are paying, and decide whether it's worth it. In India you can also hire a SEBI-registered investment adviser who charges you a fee directly and recommends direct plans.

Other costs

  • Exit load. A charge if you redeem early, often 1% if you leave an equity fund within a year. It discourages short-term trading that harms long-term holders. Not every fund has one.
  • Stamp duty. A tiny government levy on purchases (0.005% in India).
  • Trading costs inside the fund: brokerage and price impact when Ravi buys and sells. These are not in the TER, but they reduce returns. High turnover means more of them.
  • Taxes on gains, covered in Chapter 13.
Expense ratioThe fund's yearly running cost as a percentage of assets. SEBI caps it, and the cap falls as a fund grows larger. Always compare funds' TER in the same category.
Direct vs. regularSame fund, two NAVs. The direct plan's NAV is always a little higher because less has been taken out. Direct plans were introduced in India in 2013.
No entry loadIndian funds have not been allowed to charge an entry fee since 2009, so your whole investment goes in at NAV, apart from stamp duty.
Part three

Ananya invests

Chapter 8

Ananya's SIP

Ananya doesn't have a big lump sum. She has ₹5,000 every month. So she sets up a systematic investment plan, a SIP: on the 5th of every month, ₹5,000 moves automatically from her bank account into her fund, and she receives units at that day's NAV.

Her first year turns out to be wild. The market falls hard and then recovers. Watch what happens to the number of units her fixed ₹5,000 buys.

Over twelve months, she invests ₹60,000 and collects 3,952.471 units. The average NAV over the year was ₹16.00. But her average cost per unit was only ₹60,000 ÷ 3,952.471 = ₹15.18, because she automatically bought more units when they were cheap. This is called rupee cost averaging.

At the end of the year, at NAV ₹22, her units are worth ₹86,954. Had she put all ₹60,000 in at once in month 1, at ₹20, she would have 3,000 units worth ₹66,000.

That doesn't mean a SIP always wins. In a market that rises steadily, a lump sum invested early usually ends up ahead, simply because more money is invested for longer. The real strengths of a SIP are different: it matches how people actually earn (monthly), it removes the impossible job of guessing the right day, and it turns a falling market from a frightening event into a sale.

SIPA fixed amount invested at regular intervals, usually monthly, by automatic debit. Many funds accept SIPs from ₹500 or even ₹100 a month.
Step-up SIPRaising the SIP amount each year, say by 10%, as salary grows. It makes a big difference to the final amount.
STP and SWPA systematic transfer plan moves money gradually from one fund to another (say, a lump sum parked in a liquid fund into an equity fund over a year). A systematic withdrawal plan pays out a fixed amount every month (Chapter 15).
Chapter 9

Time does the heavy lifting

Meera, a retired mathematics teacher, can't resist working out where Ananya's SIP might lead. She explains compounding: returns earn returns. In the early years, almost all of Ananya's money is what she put in. In the later years, most of it is growth on growth.

She also gives Ananya the single most important number: when you start. If Ananya invests ₹5,000 a month from age 25 to 60, at 12% a year, she ends up with about ₹3.25 crore. If she waits until 35, and invests the same amount until 60, she ends up with about ₹95 lakh. Ten extra years at the start more than triples the result, even though she would have invested only ₹6 lakh more.

Try it yourself. The return is an assumption, not a promise: equity funds have had good and bad decades.

Sectors

CAGR

Compound annual growth rate: the steady yearly rate that would turn the start value into the end value. ₹1 lakh growing to ₹2 lakh in 6 years is a CAGR of 12.25%, even if the actual years went up and down.

XIRR

For SIPs, each instalment has been invested for a different time, so CAGR doesn't work. XIRR is the yearly return that accounts for every dated cash flow. Fund statements show XIRR for your own investments.

Rolling returns

Point-to-point returns depend heavily on start and end dates. Rolling returns look at every 3- or 5-year period in history, so you can see how often the fund beat its index, not just once.

Standard deviation

How much returns swing around their average. 13.8% means a typical year could be roughly 14 points above or below average. Higher means a bumpier ride.

Beta

How much the fund moves with the market. A beta of 0.92 means that when the index moves 10%, the fund has tended to move about 9.2%.

Sharpe ratio

Return earned above a safe rate, per unit of risk. (Fund return − risk-free rate) ÷ standard deviation. Higher is better. A fund returning 14% with 7% risk-free and 16% SD has a Sharpe of 0.44.

Alpha

Return beyond what the fund's beta would predict from the market's move. Positive alpha is evidence of the manager adding value, though it can also be luck over short periods.

AUM

Assets under management: the size of the fund. Too small can mean higher costs. Very large can make it harder for a mid- or small-cap fund to buy enough of small companies.

Concentration and overlap

How much is in the top 10 stocks and sectors. If you own three funds, check how many of the same stocks they hold. Owning five funds that all own the same 30 companies is not diversification.

Ananya's reading of her own fund: it lagged the index over the past year, but has beaten it over three and five years, with less volatility than the market (beta below 1). The manager has been there seven years, so those results are his. Costs are reasonable for an active fund. She decides one bad year isn't a reason to leave.

Chapter 11

The year the market fell

Go back to Ananya's first year in Chapter 8. By month 5, the NAV had halved from ₹20 to ₹10. Her ₹25,000 invested was worth only ₹17,778, down 29%. News channels were full of red arrows. People in the hospital canteen were talking about selling.

Here are three versions of what Ananya could have done.

Ananya kept going, mostly because Meera told her a story about a bridge bond whose price fell when rates rose, but which still paid every rupee in the end. The analogy isn't perfect, because shares have no maturity date and no promised payout. But the lesson holds: a fall in price is not a loss until you sell, and selling at the bottom turns a temporary fall into a permanent one.

Studies of fund investors keep finding the same thing: the average investor earns noticeably less than the funds they invest in, because they buy after prices have risen and sell after they've fallen. This is called the behaviour gap. The most valuable thing a SIP does may be to take those decisions away from you.

This doesn't mean "never sell". If your goal is a year away, money should not have been in equity to begin with. If a fund has changed its strategy, or lagged its index for years under a new manager, selling can be right. The question to ask is: has anything changed about why I bought this, apart from the price?

DrawdownThe fall from a peak to a low. Equity funds have had drawdowns of 30–60% in severe crashes. Before investing, ask yourself whether you could watch half your money vanish on paper without selling.
Market riskDiversification protects you from one company failing. It does not protect you from the whole market falling. Only time, and the right asset mix, help with that.
Chapter 12

When a debt fund broke

Ananya also kept her emergency savings, ₹1.5 lakh, in a debt fund that a colleague recommended because it paid about 2% more than others in its category. She didn't look closely at why.

The reason was in the portfolio: it held bonds from lower-rated companies, including ₹6 crore of bonds from a real-estate company rated A. One day, that company missed a payment. Its bonds were downgraded to D, the same as Dhoolgarh's in the bond story.

Three things happened at once. The fund had to value those bonds near zero, so its NAV fell 4% in a day, a shocking move for a debt fund. Investors who heard first rushed to redeem. And the fund had to sell its good bonds to pay them, because nobody would buy the bad ones, leaving remaining investors with a pot that was a little worse each day.

SEBI's rules offered a remedy called side pocketing. The fund split the defaulted bonds into a separate pocket. Everyone holding units on that day received matching units in the side pocket. The main fund, now holding only good bonds, carried on normally and could be redeemed. If the defaulted company ever paid anything, it would go to side-pocket holders, so people who rushed out later couldn't take the good assets and leave the bad ones behind.

Ananya lost about 4% of her emergency fund on paper, and got about half of that back two years later when the company's debts were restructured. Her lesson: a debt fund is a pot of bonds, and it carries every bond risk from the first story: interest-rate risk, credit risk and liquidity risk.

Credit riskBonds in the fund are downgraded or default. Check the share of the fund below AAA, and avoid credit-risk funds for emergency money.
Interest-rate riskRates rise, bond prices fall, NAV falls. A fund's modified duration tells you how much: a duration of 6 means about a 6% fall for a 1% rise in rates.
Liquidity riskThe fund can't sell its bonds quickly enough to meet redemptions without big price cuts.
Concentration riskToo much in one issuer or group. Check the top holdings.
Real eventsIn 2018 the IL&FS default hit several Indian debt funds. In April 2020, Franklin Templeton wound up six Indian debt schemes holding lower-rated bonds, freezing about ₹25,000 crore of investors' money. Most of it was eventually returned over the following years, but investors waited a long time.
YTM of a debt fundFactsheets show the portfolio's yield to maturity. A much higher YTM than similar funds usually means more credit risk or longer duration. The extra yield always comes from somewhere.
Emergency moneyKeep it in very safe, short options: savings, fixed deposits, overnight or liquid funds. Not in funds chasing extra yield.
Chapter 13

Taking money out, and tax

Five years in, Ananya wants ₹2 lakh for a deposit on a flat. To redeem, she places a request through her app. She gets that day's NAV if she's before the cut-off, minus any exit load, and the money reaches her bank in a few working days for equity funds, and usually the next working day for liquid funds.

Growth or IDCW

When she started, she had to choose between two options. In the growth option, all gains stay in the fund and show up as a rising NAV. In the IDCW option (income distribution cum capital withdrawal, formerly called "dividend"), the fund pays out money from time to time. The name was changed because these payouts are not extra income: they come out of your own NAV, which drops by the same amount. For most long-term investors, growth is simpler and more tax-efficient.

Part four

The pot in the wider world

Chapter 14

The mutual fund market

Zoom out from Ananya's single SIP. Millions of SIPs like hers, each on a different day of the month, add up to a steady river of money flowing into Indian markets every month. That river is the mutual fund market: the fund houses that make funds, the people who sell them, the investors who buy them, and the rules that hold it together.

The makers

About forty-five fund houses (AMCs) in India, from bank-owned giants to small independent firms. A handful of the largest manage over half of all the money. They compete on returns, costs, brand and the number of distributors selling their funds.

The sellers

Distributors (registered with AMFI, the industry body, and holding an ARN code), banks, brokers, online platforms and apps, and fee-only investment advisers registered with SEBI. Direct plans and apps have made it possible to invest with no one in between.

The buyers

Individual investors, increasingly from small towns like Kesarpur, plus companies parking spare cash in liquid funds, and wealthy families. Retail investors hold most of the equity fund money; companies hold much of the liquid and debt money.

The rule-makers

SEBI writes and enforces the regulations. AMFI is the industry association: it registers distributors, publishes industry data, and runs investor awareness campaigns like the "Mutual Funds Sahi Hai" adverts.

The plumbing

Registrars (CAMS, KFintech), custodians, depositories (NSDL, CDSL) for demat units, stock exchanges, which also run fund order platforms, and payment systems that make SIP debits automatic.

Why it matters to everyone

Steady SIP inflows mean Indian companies are less dependent on foreign investors, who can pull money out suddenly. Debt funds lend to companies by buying their bonds and short-term paper. The industry now holds a meaningful share of all Indian listed shares.

A short history

The idea is old. Pooled investment trusts appeared in the Netherlands and Britain in the 1700s and 1800s. The first modern open-ended fund, buying and selling units at net asset value every day, began in Boston in 1924. In India, the government set up the Unit Trust of India in 1963, and for decades it was the only mutual fund. Public-sector banks were allowed in from 1987 and private fund houses from 1993, the same year the first SEBI mutual fund regulations were written; the current regulations date from 1996. UTI's flagship scheme, US-64, ran into a crisis in 2001 because it had promised returns its investments couldn't support, a lesson in why funds today aren't allowed to promise returns. The industry has since grown many times over, helped by direct plans, online KYC and the spread of SIPs.

How a mutual fund compares with other ways to invest

Chapter 15

A plan for three generations

One Sunday, three generations sit around Meera's kitchen table: Meera, now 82; Ananya, now 30; and Ananya's newborn son. Meera makes them do what she made her students do: write down the goal before choosing the method.

Rule one: match the pot to the time. Money needed within three years shouldn't be in equity, because a crash could hit just when it's needed. Money needed in ten years or more can ride out crashes, and has the most to gain from equity's long-run growth.

Rule two: decide the mix, then rebalance. Ananya chooses 70% equity, 30% debt for her retirement money. If a great year pushes equity to 80%, she moves some back to debt; after a crash, she moves some from debt into equity. Rebalancing forces her to sell some of whatever has risen and buy some of whatever has fallen, the opposite of what fear and greed suggest.

Rule three: keep it simple. Two to four funds can cover almost any plan: perhaps an index fund, a flexi cap fund, a short-duration debt fund and a liquid fund. Owning twelve funds usually means owning the same companies twelve times, with more paperwork.

Rule four: income in retirement. Meera sets up a systematic withdrawal plan: ₹8,000 on the 1st of each month from her conservative hybrid fund. If the fund earns more than she withdraws over time, her capital lasts; if she withdraws too much, it runs down. She sets the withdrawal at about 5% a year of her capital, leaving room for bad years.

Rule five: insurance first. Before investing for growth, Ananya buys simple term life insurance and health insurance, so a hospital bill or the worst case doesn't force her to sell at a bad time.

Asset allocationHow you split money between equity, debt, gold and cash. Research suggests it explains most of the ups and downs in a portfolio, more than which particular funds you choose.
Glide pathGradually moving from equity to debt as a goal approaches, so a late crash can't wreck it. Some retirement funds do this automatically.
Review, don't fiddleCheck once or twice a year: Is the mix right? Is each fund still doing its job against its benchmark and category? Otherwise, leave it alone.
Chapter 16

Twenty years on

Ananya is now 45 and runs the nursing staff at the hospital, the job Sister Thomas once had. Her SIP started at ₹5,000 and rose with each pay rise. She lived through three big market falls and stopped her SIP in none of them. She switched funds twice: once when a manager left and the fund drifted from its stated style, and once to move her son's education money into debt as his college years approached.

The pot that started as a canteen conversation turned out to have everything Sister Thomas wanted: one share of a thousand decisions made by professionals, guarded by a structure in which no one could run off with the money, priced fairly every evening, and open to anyone with a few hundred rupees.

It also carried every risk the nurses feared: markets that fall, managers who get things wrong, costs that compound quietly, and bonds that don't pay. A mutual fund doesn't remove those risks. It shares them, spreads them, and makes them visible, so that an ordinary saver can take them on knowingly.

That is a mutual fund: a common pot, fairly divided. And the mutual fund market is the system that lets millions of people like Ananya own a small piece of their country's companies and bonds, and grow along with them.

The two stories togetherIn the bond story, Kesarpur borrowed to build a bridge. In this one, savers pool money to own companies and bonds. Mutual funds connect the two: many debt funds hold exactly the kind of bonds Meera once bought.
After the story

Eight myths about mutual funds

A lower NAV is cheaper.NAV is just the value of one slice. ₹10 units and ₹500 units in identical funds grow by the same percentage. A new fund at ₹10 is not a bargain.
Mutual funds only invest in shares.Many hold only bonds, money-market loans, or gold. Check what's in the pot.
Debt funds are as safe as an FD.They carry interest-rate, credit and liquidity risk (Chapter 12), and they aren't insured.
A SIP guarantees profit.A SIP is a way of investing, not a product. It averages your cost; it doesn't stop the market falling.
More funds mean more diversification.Several funds in the same category often hold the same stocks. Two or three well-chosen funds can be more diversified than ten.
Last year's top fund will win next year.Top rankings change often. Look at long, rolling records, costs and process, not a single year.
IDCW is free extra income.Payouts come out of your own NAV, and are taxed at your slab rate.
You need a lot of money to start.Many funds accept SIPs from ₹100–500 a month.

Before you invest: a checklist

  • I know what this money is for, and when I'll need it.
  • I have an emergency fund and health insurance in place first.
  • The fund's category matches my timeline (equity only for 5+ years).
  • I've read the riskometer and the fund's objective in the SID or KIM.
  • I've compared its expense ratio with others in the category, and chosen direct or regular knowingly.
  • I've looked at 3-, 5- and 10-year and rolling returns against its benchmark, not just last year.
  • For a debt fund, I've checked credit quality, duration and YTM.
  • I know the exit load and lock-in, if any.
  • I won't check the NAV every day, and I won't sell just because the market fell.
  • I'll review once or twice a year and rebalance if my mix drifts.
