A story about insurance, in sixteen chapters

The Kesarpur Safety Net

When a bus depot mechanic dies in an accident, his family is left with a loan, two children and no income. His friend Suresh, the bus conductor, goes home and asks a question every family should ask: what would happen to mine? Follow Suresh as he learns how insurance works, what it's for, what it isn't for, and how to buy the right kind without being sold the wrong kind.

Part one

How insurance works

Prologue

What happened to Raju's family

Suresh has been a conductor on the Kesarpur–district headquarters bus for twelve years. In the bond story he bought a zero-coupon bond for his baby daughter, Priya. She's now in school, and her brother Arjun is four.

Last month, Raju, a mechanic at the bus depot and Suresh's friend, was killed when a truck hit his motorcycle. He was 36. He left his wife, two children, a ₹4 lakh loan for his house extension, and almost no savings. The depot staff collected ₹60,000. Raju's wife now works as a cook in three houses, and the eldest child has left school.

Raju had one insurance policy: a small "money-back" plan an agent sold him, which paid ₹1.5 lakh. It was gone in four months.

That night, Suresh does the sum he has always avoided. If he died tomorrow, what would Leela and the children have? The answer frightens him. The next morning, he starts learning about insurance.

Tenth storyThis follows the nine market stories. Suresh, Ananya, Gopal, Harjeet, Kamala and Iyer's bank all appear.
Protection firstEvery earlier story assumed nothing terrible happens while you save and invest. Insurance is what protects the plan when something does.
Chapter 1

The pool

At the depot canteen, Ananya, the nurse from the fund story, explains it the way her hospital's finance officer once explained it to her.

Ananya"Imagine a thousand depot workers. Each year, about one in a hundred has a serious accident or illness that costs ₹2 lakh. Nobody knows who. If each of you saves for it alone, you need ₹2 lakh sitting idle. But if all thousand put ₹2,000 a year into one pot, the pot has ₹20 lakh, enough to pay the ten who get hurt. Everyone swaps a big, unknown risk for a small, known cost."

That's insurance: pooling risk. Each person pays a small premium, and the pool pays the few who suffer a loss. It works because of the law of large numbers: you can't predict what happens to one person, but across thousands of people the total becomes very predictable. Try it.

PremiumThe price you pay for insurance, usually yearly or monthly.
Law of large numbersAs a group grows, its actual average loss gets closer and closer to the expected average. That's what lets an insurer set a fair price.
An old ideaMerchants shared shipping risks thousands of years ago. Modern insurance grew from London's marine insurers in the 1600s and 1700s.
Chapter 2

Inside an insurance company

An insurance company runs the pool professionally. Here's what happens to Suresh's premium.

  • Actuaries use data on deaths, illness and accidents to calculate how much each kind of person is likely to cost. A 30-year-old non-smoker costs less to cover than a 50-year-old smoker.
  • Underwriters check each application: age, health, job, habits. They may ask for medical tests, charge more, exclude something, or decline.
  • Investments. Premiums arrive long before most claims. Insurers invest this money, mostly in government bonds and other safe assets. Insurance companies are among the biggest buyers of long-term bonds in India.
  • Reinsurance. To avoid being ruined by one huge disaster, insurers pass part of their risk to reinsurers, insurers for insurers.
  • Regulation. The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) licenses insurers, sets capital rules so they can pay claims, approves products, and handles complaints.
Adverse selectionPeople who expect to claim are keenest to buy. That's why insurers ask health questions, and why hiding a condition can void a policy.
Moral hazardBeing insured can make people less careful. Deductibles and co-payments keep some cost with the policyholder for that reason.
Chapter 3

Insure the big, not the small

Should Suresh insure his phone? His bus pass? Iyer, the bank treasurer from the money market story, gives him a simple test: insure what would wreck you, not what would merely annoy you.

Self-insurePaying small losses yourself from an emergency fund. Cheaper over time than insuring them.
Avoid and reduceSome risks are better avoided or reduced: a helmet, a smoke detector, a seat belt. Insurance pays after the loss; prevention stops it.
Part two

Life and health

Chapter 4

How much life cover?

Life insurance pays a lump sum to your family if you die during the policy. It isn't for you; it replaces your income for the people who depend on it. The key question is how much they'd need.

Suresh lists it: the family spends about ₹2.5 lakh a year that his salary pays for; he wants to cover that until Arjun is about 25; there's a ₹5 lakh loan; and he wants ₹15 lakh for the children's education. He has ₹3 lakh in savings and his provident fund.

Cover needed₹65.8 lakh
Rounded up₹75 lakh

Expenses are valued assuming the payout is invested to earn about 2% more than inflation. A common rough rule is 10–15 times yearly income; a calculation like this is better.

Who needs life coverAnyone whose income others depend on. A single person with no dependants and no loans may need little or none. Children almost never need it.
Review itNeed is highest when children are young and loans are large, and falls as savings grow and children become independent.
Chapter 5

Term, endowment, ULIP

Mr. Malhotra, the agent who sold Raju his policy, visits Suresh. He recommends an endowment plan: ₹50,000 a year for 20 years, life cover of ₹10 lakh, and "guaranteed money back at maturity, nothing wasted". Suresh compares it with a term plan, which pays only if he dies during the term, and returns nothing if he survives.

Then Suresh tries the alternative people call "buy term and invest the rest": buy ₹1 crore of term cover for about ₹12,000 a year, and put the other ₹38,000 into a mutual fund SIP.

The term route gives ten times the protection for the same yearly spend, and the maturity money is comparable or better at moderate returns. The endowment's "nothing wasted" feeling comes at the cost of far too little cover, which is exactly what failed Raju's family. Endowments also lose heavily if you stop paying early, because surrender values in the first years are low.

Term insurance works only with discipline: the "invest the rest" part has to actually happen. For people who know they won't invest on their own, a combined plan may still be better than nothing, but they should know what they're paying for.

Illustrative numbersTerm premiums depend on age, health, habits and insurer. For a healthy 30-something, ₹1 crore of cover often costs roughly ₹10,000–15,000 a year online.
Agents' commissionsCommissions on endowment and ULIP plans are usually much higher than on term plans, which partly explains what gets pushed.
Chapter 6

The hospital bill

Ananya works at the district hospital and sees insurance from the other side every day. She tells Suresh that for most families, the bigger financial danger isn't death; it's a serious illness. A heart surgery, a cancer treatment or a long ICU stay can cost several lakh rupees, and push a family into debt for years.

A health insurance policy pays hospital bills up to a sum insured each year. Here's what happened when Leela was admitted for gallbladder surgery the following year, using a ₹5 lakh family floater policy.

Family floater

One sum insured shared by the whole family. Cheaper than separate policies, but one big claim can use it all up for everyone that year.

Super top-up

Extra cover that starts once a yearly total of bills crosses a set amount (the deductible). A cheap way to add ₹10–20 lakh of cover on top of a base policy.

No-claim bonus

Many policies raise the sum insured each claim-free year, with no extra premium.

Network hospitalA hospital with an agreement with your insurer or its TPA for cashless treatment. Check the list before choosing a policy.
Pre- and post-hospitalisationMost policies also cover tests and medicines for a set number of days before and after a hospital stay.
Chapter 7

The room-rent trap

Leela's bill came to ₹3 lakh. But the insurer paid less than Suresh expected. The reason was one line in the policy: a room rent limit of 1% of the sum insured per day, ₹5,000. The hospital had only a private room at ₹8,000 a day free, so they took it.

Under many policies, choosing a room above the limit doesn't just cost you the extra rent. Charges linked to the room category, like doctors' visits, nursing and some procedures, are cut in the same proportion. This is called a proportionate deduction.

Bill₹3,00,000
Insurer pays₹2,28,000
Suresh pays₹72,000

Illustrative: 4 days; linked charges ₹1.6 lakh; medicines and implants ₹1.08 lakh, usually paid in full. Rules on proportionate deduction vary by policy and have been tightened by IRDAI for some charges.

The lesson: when buying, prefer policies with no room rent limit or a "single private room" allowance. When admitted, ask which room category your policy covers before choosing.

Other cost-sharingCo-payment: you pay a fixed share of every claim (common in senior citizen policies). Sub-limits: caps on specific treatments like cataract surgery. Deductible: you pay the first amount yourself.
Chapter 8

Reading the fine print

Suresh reads his health policy wording carefully for the first time. He finds:

Initial waiting periodIllnesses (not accidents) in the first 30 days usually aren't covered.
Specific diseasesSome conditions, such as cataract, hernia or joint replacement, have their own waiting period, often 1–2 years.
Pre-existing diseasesConditions you already have, like diabetes or high blood pressure, are covered only after a waiting period. Since 2024, IRDAI has capped this at three years.
ExclusionsCosmetic surgery, some fertility treatments, self-inflicted injuries and certain other items aren't covered. They're listed in the policy.
DisclosureYou must truthfully declare your health, habits and existing illnesses when applying. Hiding them is the most common reason claims are refused.

He also learns three things that protect him:

  • Free-look period: after receiving a new policy, you can return it within a set period, now 30 days for most policies, and get most of the premium back.
  • Portability: you can move a health policy to another insurer and keep the waiting period you've already served.
  • Claim settlement data: insurers publish what share of claims they pay. A higher ratio, and fewer complaints, is a good sign, though it's only one measure.
Policy wordingThe full legal document. The brochure summarises; the wording decides claims.
Tell the truthDisclose every condition and habit, even if it raises the premium. A cheaper policy that won't pay is worthless.
Part three

Things, crops and schemes

Chapter 9

Vehicles, shops and fire

Harjeet, the petrol pump owner from the commodity story, insures very different things: his tanker truck, his pump's buildings and stock, and his liability if someone is hurt on his premises. This is general insurance: cover for property and liability, usually renewed every year.

Motor insurance

Third-party cover is compulsory by law for every vehicle on Indian roads; it pays for injury or damage you cause to others. Own-damage cover pays for your vehicle. The vehicle's insured value (IDV) falls with age.

Fire and property

Covers buildings, machinery and stock against fire, lightning, floods, storms and some other perils. Harjeet adds cover for business interruption, to replace lost income while the pump is shut after a fire.

Liability

Pays if he's legally responsible for harm to customers or the public. Many businesses also buy workers' compensation cover for employees.

Two ideas save Harjeet money. A deductible: he agrees to pay the first ₹25,000 of any claim, and his premium drops. And the no-claim bonus on motor insurance: a discount that grows each claim-free year, so he pays small repairs himself rather than claiming and losing it.

He also learns about under-insurance. If his stock is worth ₹50 lakh but he insures it for ₹25 lakh, the insurer may pay only half of any partial loss, because he insured only half its value. This is called the average clause.

IndemnityGeneral insurance aims to put you back where you were before the loss, not to let you profit from it.
Chapter 10

Insurance for a crop

Gopal, the farmer from the derivatives and commodity stories, faces risks no warehouse or futures contract can fix: drought, hail, floods and pests that destroy the crop itself. For these, India runs the Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY), launched in 2016.

  • Farmers pay a small share of the premium: 2% for kharif crops, 1.5% for rabi crops, and 5% for commercial and horticultural crops. Central and state governments pay the rest.
  • For widespread losses, claims are usually based on crop-cutting experiments measuring the average yield in an area, compared with a threshold. Individual farms can claim for some localised disasters.
  • Some states use weather-based insurance, which pays when rainfall or temperature at a weather station crosses set levels, without assessing each field.

It's far from perfect: delayed payouts, disputes over yield measurements, and farmers unaware they were enrolled. But for Gopal's group, it means a hailstorm in February no longer means losing the land.

Price vs. yieldThe derivatives story protected against a fall in price. Crop insurance protects against a fall in quantity. Gopal now uses both.
Basis riskArea-based and weather-based payouts may not match one farmer's actual loss.
Chapter 11

Government schemes

Iyer shows Suresh three low-cost government-backed schemes he can join through his bank account. Premiums shown are as revised in 2022; check current terms.

Raju's family would have received ₹4 lakh from these two small policies, for less than ₹500 a year. Suresh enrols that week. They're a valuable floor, but ₹2 lakh is far below what his family needs, so he treats them as an addition to proper term and health cover, not a replacement.

Auto-debitThese schemes renew by automatic debit from your bank account each year. Keep enough balance on the renewal date, or the cover lapses.
Chapter 12

When a claim is refused

Ananya sees claims refused every month at the hospital. The most common reasons: an undisclosed earlier illness, a treatment excluded or still in its waiting period, missing documents, or a policy that had lapsed because a premium wasn't paid. Some refusals are simply wrong.

When a claim is refused unfairly, there's a ladder of complaints, each step free or low-cost:

Insurance OmbudsmanAn independent official who hears individual complaints about insurers free of charge, within monetary limits.
NomineeAlways name a nominee in every policy and keep the family informed where the documents are. Many claims are delayed because families don't know a policy exists.
Chapter 13

Kamala's "fixed deposit"

Kamala, whose investment mistakes run through these stories, went to her bank to renew a fixed deposit. A friendly staff member told her about a "better FD": "Pay ₹1 lakh a year for five years, get a big amount after ten, plus insurance." She signed.

It wasn't a fixed deposit. It was a ten-year endowment policy from the bank's partner insurer. When she needed money in the third year, the surrender value was far below what she'd paid. This is mis-selling, and policies sold at bank branches have been a frequent source of such complaints.

"It's like an FD"An FD is a bank deposit. A policy is insurance. Ask: "Is this an insurance policy? From which insurer?"
"Guaranteed returns"Ask for the exact guaranteed amount in writing, and work out the yearly return. It's often 4–6%, below inflation after tax rules and charges.
"Only five years"Paying for five years doesn't mean the money comes back in five years. Check the maturity date and the surrender value table.
Fake callsCallers claiming to be from IRDAI or offering "bonus" on old policies are frauds. IRDAI doesn't sell policies or call about bonuses.

Had Kamala noticed within the free-look period, she could have returned the policy. Rohit helps her file a complaint about the mis-selling with the insurer, and escalates to the grievance portal.

Benefit illustrationInsurers must give a document showing projected benefits at different return assumptions. Read the lower one, and the surrender values.
Chapter 14

The insurance market at scale

Zoom out from Kesarpur. India's insurance market has grown quickly, but insurance premiums are still only around 4% of the country's GDP, well below many other countries. Many families, like Raju's, are badly under-insured: they have some policy, but far too little cover.

Life insurers

LIC, created in 1956 when life insurance was nationalised, is still the largest. Private life insurers have been allowed since 2000.

General and health insurers

Public-sector general insurers, private general insurers, and standalone health insurers covering motor, property, health, travel, crop and more.

Distribution

Agents, banks (bancassurance), brokers, online aggregators and direct online sales. Online term plans are usually cheapest because there's no agent commission.

Reinsurers

The state-owned GIC Re and global reinsurers take on part of insurers' largest risks.

Regulator and redress

IRDAI regulates insurers and intermediaries, the Bima Bharosa portal handles complaints, and Insurance Ombudsmen resolve disputes.

Insurers as investors

Insurers hold enormous long-term investments, especially government bonds. They are a major source of the long-term money that funds roads, bridges and companies in the other stories.

Chapter 15

Suresh's family plan

Combining everything, Suresh and Leela build their family's safety net in order. Each layer protects the one above it.

TaxUnder the old tax regime, health premiums can be deducted under Section 80D, within limits. The new regime, now the default, doesn't allow this. Tax should never be the main reason to buy insurance.
Chapter 16

Five years later

Five years later, Arjun breaks his leg badly falling from a wall. The surgery and stay cost ₹2.6 lakh. The insurer pays it cashless; Suresh pays only for a few excluded items. A year after that, Gopal's mustard is hit by hail, and the crop insurance payout covers most of his loan. Harjeet's tanker is damaged in a collision; the insurer pays for repairs and the other driver's injuries.

Most years, though, nothing happens, and the premiums simply go. Suresh used to feel that was waste. Now he sees it differently: each year he paid for the certainty that Leela and the children would be all right whatever happened. Raju's family's story will never be theirs.

Suresh also helped start a small fund at the depot for Raju's children's schooling, and every new depot worker now gets a one-page guide to term cover, health cover and the government schemes.

That is what insurance is: many people paying small, certain amounts so that the few who suffer large, uncertain losses aren't ruined. And that is what the insurance market is: the insurers, reinsurers, regulators and distributors that run those pools, for lives, health, vehicles, shops and crops, and invest the money in between.

The ten storiesBonds lend. Funds pool. Shares own. Derivatives move risk. The money market keeps cash flowing. Forex crosses borders. Commodities price real goods. Real estate builds homes. Crypto tests trust. Insurance protects all of it.
After the story

Eight myths about insurance

Term insurance wastes money if I survive.You paid for protection and had it. That's the product working.
Insurance is a good investment.Combined plans give too little cover and modest returns. Keep insurance and investing separate.
My employer's health cover is enough.It ends when the job ends, often when you need it most. Keep your own policy too.
₹2–3 lakh health cover is enough.One major surgery or ICU stay can exceed it. A super top-up adds cover cheaply.
Small details on the form don't matter.Non-disclosure is the most common reason claims are refused.
Children need life insurance.Life cover replaces income. Children usually have none to replace.
A bank officer selling a "plan" is selling an FD.Ask directly whether it's an insurance policy.
All health policies are the same.Room-rent limits, co-pays, sub-limits and waiting periods make huge differences.

Before buying insurance: a checklist

  • I've calculated how much life cover my dependants need.
  • I'm buying term cover for protection, separately from investments.
  • My family has health cover of its own, not only through an employer.
  • I've checked room-rent limits, co-pays, sub-limits and waiting periods.
  • The hospitals I'd use are in the insurer's network.
  • I've disclosed every health condition and habit truthfully.
  • I've named nominees and told my family where the documents are.
  • I've read the policy wording, not just the brochure, within the free-look period.
  • Premiums are on auto-pay so the cover can't lapse.
  • I'll review my cover after marriage, children, a home loan or a new job.
