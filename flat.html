The Kesarpur Flat

Ananya's landlord has raised the rent again. She's thirty, has some savings, and everyone tells her to "stop paying someone else's EMI". Should she buy a ₹40 lakh flat on the far side of the bridge? Follow her search, her loan and her paperwork, and you'll understand how land and buildings are priced, financed, traded and owned.

Part one

Choosing a home

Prologue

Another rent increase

Ananya, the nurse from the mutual fund story, rents a two-bedroom flat near the hospital for ₹12,000 a month. This month her landlord raised it by 8%, the third increase in four years. Her colleagues have started buying flats in the new towers on the far side of the river, near the bridge Kesarpur built with bonds years ago.

Her SIP has grown well. She has about ₹16 lakh in savings and mutual funds. Her aunt says, "Rent is money thrown away. Buy." Her grandmother Meera, the retired mathematics teacher, says, "Do the sums first."

A flat is likely to be the biggest purchase of Ananya's life, and the hardest to undo. It's also unlike everything else in these stories: you can live in it, it can't be moved, no two are exactly alike, and selling one can take months. This is the real estate market.

Household wealthIndian households have traditionally kept most of their wealth in property and gold. An RBI committee in 2017 estimated that real estate made up around three-quarters of the average household's assets.
Chapter 1

What real estate is

Real estate means land and anything permanently built on it: houses, flats, shops, offices, warehouses, farms. The law calls it immovable property. It's different from the other things in these stories in five ways.

  • Every property is unique. Two flats in the same tower differ in floor, view, sunlight and noise. There's no single "price of a flat" the way there is a price of gold.
  • It's illiquid. You can sell a share in a second. Selling a flat can take months, with agents, buyers' loans and paperwork.
  • It's expensive to trade. Stamp duty, registration, brokerage and taxes can add up to 10% of the price to buy and sell.
  • It's usually bought with borrowed money, which magnifies both gains and losses (Chapter 9).
  • It does two jobs. It gives a place to live or work (or rent income), and it may rise in value.
Primary and resaleBuying from a builder is the primary market; buying from a previous owner is resale, the secondary market.
Chapter 2

Location, and the bridge

Meera remembers the far bank before the bridge: fields, a few houses, land at about ₹400 a square foot that nobody wanted. Then the bridge opened. The hospital and station were six minutes away instead of fifty. Builders came. Fifteen years later, land near the bridge sells for about ₹3,000 a square foot.

The buildings on that land are ordinary concrete, which costs roughly the same to put up anywhere in the district. What changed was the land, and what made the land valuable was everything around it: the bridge, the roads, jobs, schools, water.

So, before choosing a flat, Ananya lists what makes a location valuable to her, and to whoever might rent or buy it from her later: distance to work, the hospital and schools; water supply and drainage (does the area flood?); roads and public transport; planned infrastructure; and the neighbourhood itself.

Location, location, locationThe old saying is really about land. You can rebuild a building; you can't move it.
InfrastructureNew roads, metro lines, bridges and airports often raise nearby land prices, sometimes years before they open, when they're announced. That's why plans and rumours move land prices.
Chapter 3

How big is the flat, really?

The brochure from Mr. Deshmukh's tower says "900 sq ft". Inside, the rooms feel smaller. That's because there are three different areas:

  • Carpet area: the floor space inside your walls where you could lay a carpet. This is the space you actually live in.
  • Built-up area: carpet area plus the thickness of walls and your balcony.
  • Super built-up area: built-up area plus your share of common spaces: corridors, lifts, stairs, lobby, clubhouse.

Because the same flat can be described three ways, buyers were long confused. Under the real estate law of 2016, RERA, builders must state the price and size of a flat in carpet area. Ananya always compares price per square foot of carpet area.

LoadingHow much the super built-up area exceeds the carpet area, often 25–40% in towers with many common facilities.
Chapter 4

Under construction or ready?

Mr. Deshmukh's tower is half built. He offers Ananya a flat at ₹40 lakh, about 10% cheaper than a similar ready flat next door, with payments spread over two years as floors are completed. A cheaper price, and time to pay. What's the catch?

The catch is that she's lending money to a builder against a promise. In the 2010s, thousands of Indian families paid for flats in projects that were delayed for years or never finished, when builders ran short of money or spent buyers' payments on other projects. That's why Parliament passed the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016.

What RERA requires

Projects above a minimum size must register with the state RERA authority, publish approvals, plans and timelines, sell by carpet area, keep 70% of buyers' money in the project account, pay interest to buyers for delays, and fix structural defects reported within five years of handover.

Under construction

Lower price and staggered payments. But risk of delay, changes, or a stalled project. GST applies (5% on most homes, 1% on affordable ones, without input credit). Ananya must keep paying rent until handover.

Ready to move

What you see is what you get. Move in immediately and stop paying rent. No GST if the builder has the completion certificate. But a higher price, and fewer choices.

Ananya checks the state RERA website: Deshmukh's project is registered, his previous two towers were delivered within six months of schedule, and the land approvals are listed. She decides to go ahead, but she'll pay only according to the construction-linked schedule, never large sums upfront.

Chapter 5

Who really owns it?

When you buy a share, the depository guarantees who owns it. Land is different. Ownership in India rests on a chain of documents going back decades, and gaps or disputes in that chain are common. A lawyer's title search checks that the seller really has the right to sell.

Ananya's lawyer checks:

  • Title deeds for every past transfer, all registered.
  • An encumbrance certificate from the sub-registrar, showing any mortgages or claims on the land.
  • Land records and mutation in the revenue records, showing the current owner for land tax.
  • Land-use conversion from agricultural to residential, and approved building plans.
  • On completion, the occupancy certificate confirming the building is fit to live in.

A bank lending against the flat does its own legal check, which is another layer of protection. That's one reason Ananya is glad to borrow from Iyer's bank.

Power of attorney salesSome properties change hands through "general power of attorney" documents instead of registered sale deeds. The Supreme Court has held that these don't transfer ownership. Buy only through a registered sale deed.
Circle rateThe minimum value set by the state for each area, used to calculate stamp duty. Registering a sale below it can trigger extra tax.
Part two

Paying for it

Chapter 6

The real price of a flat

The flat is priced at ₹40 lakh. But that's not what Ananya will pay. She adds every cost.

Total cost to move in₹48.4 lakh
Above the agreement price+21%

Many states charge women buyers a lower stamp duty, often 1% less, to encourage women's ownership. Ananya registers the flat in her own name and saves ₹40,000.

The lesson: the "price" is only the start. Interiors, furniture, deposits, and costs like a car park or club membership can add far more than people expect. And most of these costs are gone the moment she buys. If she sold the next day, she'd lose them.

Stamp dutyA state tax on the sale deed, usually 5–7% of the price or circle rate, whichever is higher. Registration adds about 1%, with caps in some states.
Cash paymentsNever agree to pay part of the price in unrecorded cash. It's illegal, it can't be financed by a loan, and you'll have no proof of it later.
Chapter 7

The home loan

Ananya can't pay ₹40 lakh herself. She goes to Iyer at the cooperative bank. He explains the rules:

  • The RBI limits how much of a home's value banks can lend, the loan-to-value (LTV) ratio: up to 90% for loans up to ₹30 lakh, 80% for ₹30–75 lakh, and 75% above that. For her ₹40 lakh flat, the bank lends up to ₹32 lakh. She pays ₹8 lakh, the down payment, plus all the extra costs from Chapter 6, herself.
  • Her loan has a floating rate linked to the RBI's repo rate (the money market story explains why). If the RBI raises rates, her rate rises.
  • The bank checks that her EMI won't be more than about 40–50% of her take-home pay.
  • She can prepay a floating-rate home loan at any time without penalty.

Each month she pays an equated monthly instalment (EMI). Early on, most of it is interest; later, most of it repays the loan.

Over 20 years, Ananya will pay back more than twice what she borrowed. In the first year alone, about ₹2.7 lakh of her ₹3.3 lakh in EMIs is interest. That's why even small prepayments early in the loan save a lot, and why a 1% change in rates matters so much. Try moving the rate from 8.5% to 9.5%.

When rates riseBanks usually keep the EMI the same and extend the loan's length, sometimes by years. Ask the bank to raise the EMI instead, if you can afford it.
InsuranceBanks often push loan insurance policies. A simple term life policy covering the loan amount is usually much cheaper.
Chapter 8

Rent or buy?

Now Meera's sums. "Rent is money thrown away," the aunt said. But interest is also money thrown away, and so are stamp duty and maintenance. The fair comparison is between two paths:

  • Buy: pay the down payment and costs, then EMIs and maintenance. End up owning a flat, minus any loan left.
  • Rent and invest: keep paying rent, and put the down payment, the costs, and every month's difference between owning costs and rent into mutual funds.

Rent in India is low compared with property prices: Ananya's ₹12,000 a month on a ₹40 lakh flat is a rental yield of only 3.6% a year, less than half her loan rate. That tilts the sums toward renting, as long as she really invests the difference. Change the assumptions and see which path ends up with more wealth.

Buy: flat value − loan left − 2% selling costRent and invest the difference

The answer moves a lot with assumptions nobody can know: how fast prices and rents will grow, and what investments will return. And it ignores things that matter more to many people: the security of not being asked to leave, freedom to renovate, and the discipline an EMI forces. Renting and investing only wins if you actually invest every rupee of the difference, every month, for decades. Most people don't.

Ananya decides to buy, for security and because she plans to stay at the hospital for many years. But she buys a flat she can afford with an EMI of under 40% of her pay, and keeps her SIP going.

Rental yieldYearly rent ÷ property price. Indian homes often yield 2–4%, low by global standards, because buyers expect price growth.
Price-to-rentPrice ÷ yearly rent. Ananya's flat: ₹40 lakh ÷ ₹1.44 lakh ≈ 28. The higher this number, the stronger the case for renting.
Chapter 9

Leverage, the double edge

Property feels safe, but almost everyone buys it with borrowed money. That's the same leverage as Rohit's futures in the derivatives story, just slower.

With 20% down, a 10% rise in the flat's price raises Ananya's own stake by 50%. That's why property has made many families wealthy. But a 10% fall cuts her stake in half, and a 20% fall wipes it out: she'd owe the bank as much as the flat is worth. In the US housing crash after 2006, millions of families ended up owing more than their homes were worth.

These figures ignore stamp duty and other costs, which make real outcomes worse. After paying 10% or more in costs, a flat has to rise noticeably just to break even.

EquityIn property, the part you own outright: value minus loan outstanding.
Negative equityOwing more than the property is worth. You can't sell without bringing extra money.
Part three

Property as an investment

Chapter 10

Kamala's plot

Kamala, Rohit's great-aunt, has a property story too, and it's the cautionary one. Fifteen years ago, an agent sold her a plot in a "new township" ten kilometres out of town. Glossy brochure, a gate, a few roads marked with lime. "The highway is coming," he said. "Prices will triple."

She paid ₹6 lakh, part of it in cash "to save stamp duty". Here's what she learned.

Unapproved layoutThe land had never been converted from agricultural use, and the layout wasn't approved by the town planning authority. No bank would lend to buyers, and no building permit could be issued.
Cash and undervaluationBecause she registered a lower price, her official purchase cost is low. If she ever sells, she'll pay more tax on the "gain", and the cash part can't be proven.
IlliquidityThe highway took a different route. Buyers vanished. She's tried to sell for six years. There's no market price for a plot nobody wants.
EncroachmentWhen she visited after a few years, someone had fenced part of her plot. Land you don't watch can be occupied, and evicting someone through the courts can take years.
Holding costsProperty tax, fencing, travel to check on it. An empty plot earns nothing while it waits.
Kamala"Everyone says land never loses value. Maybe land in general doesn't. My piece of land did."
Before buying landApproved layout? Land-use conversion? Clear title? Road access? Registered at full value? Would a bank lend against it? If not, ask why.
Benami propertyProperty held in someone else's name to hide the real owner is illegal under India's benami law and can be confiscated.
Chapter 11

The landlord's side

Ananya's old landlord, Mr. Kulkarni, bought her rented flat as an investment. Here are his real numbers for a year.

A net yield of about 2% is less than a savings account. Mr. Kulkarni is really betting on the flat's price rising. When prices are flat for years, as they were in many Indian cities in the late 2010s, landlords earn very little.

Being a landlord also means work and risk: finding tenants, collecting rent, handling repairs, and resolving disputes. Most rentals use 11-month agreements, partly because leases of a year or more must be registered. A model tenancy law proposed by the central government in 2021 aims to make renting fairer and faster for both sides, but states must adopt it.

Gross vs. net yieldGross: rent ÷ price. Net: rent minus all costs and vacancy ÷ price. Always ask for the net.
Commercial propertyOffices and shops often earn higher yields, 6–9% gross, with longer leases, but need larger sums and carry vacancy risk if a tenant leaves.
Chapter 12

Booms, busts and stalled towers

Property prices move in long, slow cycles, often lasting a decade or more. Because building takes years, supply always arrives late, after prices have already risen.

Busts can be severe. In Japan, land and property prices soared in the late 1980s and then fell for more than a decade after 1990. In the US, easy mortgages to borrowers who couldn't repay fuelled a boom that collapsed after 2006; house prices fell by about a quarter nationally, and the losses on mortgage securities triggered the 2008 financial crisis. In China, the developer Evergrande defaulted in 2021 with huge debts and many unfinished flats, part of a wider property slump.

India's cycle has been different but painful. After a boom in the late 2000s and early 2010s, prices in many cities stagnated for years. Some large developers collapsed, leaving tens of thousands of families with half-built flats and EMIs to pay. Courts, bankruptcy proceedings (homebuyers were recognised as financial creditors in 2018), and government funds for stalled projects have helped some finish. RERA was a direct response to this period.

Unsold inventoryFlats built but not sold. When it piles up, prices stall and builders offer discounts and freebies.
Price indicesThe RBI's house price index and the National Housing Bank's RESIDEX track prices across cities, though property prices are harder to measure than share prices.
Chapter 13

Owning a mall without a brick

Rohit wants some property in his investments, but he doesn't want a second flat, a tenant or a loan. Neha, his analyst friend, suggests a REIT: a real estate investment trust.

A REIT is like a mutual fund for buildings. It owns completed, rent-earning properties such as office parks and malls, collects rent from businesses, and passes most of the cash to unitholders. Its units trade on the stock exchange like shares.

The rules

SEBI's REIT rules (2014) require at least 80% of assets to be completed, income-producing property, and at least 90% of distributable cash to be paid out. The first Indian REIT listed in 2019, and units can now be bought one at a time on the exchange.

Why Rohit likes it

Small amounts, no stamp duty, professional management, diversified tenants, regular payouts, and he can sell in seconds.

The risks

Prices move daily with interest rates and sentiment. Offices can empty if companies shrink or work from home. Payouts aren't guaranteed, and different parts of the payout are taxed differently.

InvITsInfrastructure investment trusts work the same way for toll roads, power lines and pipelines.
Small REITsSince 2024, SEBI has allowed small and medium REITs, for smaller property portfolios, with their own rules.
Chapter 14

The real estate market at scale

Zoom out from Kesarpur. Real estate is one of India's biggest industries and employers, linked to cement, steel, paint, furniture, banking and millions of construction jobs. Here's who's involved.

Developers

From national listed companies to local builders like Deshmukh. They buy land, get approvals, raise money from banks, investors and buyers' advances, and build.

Lenders

Banks and housing finance companies lend to buyers (home loans) and to developers (construction finance). The RBI and the National Housing Bank oversee them.

Government

Sets land-use and building rules, collects stamp duty (a major source of state revenue), sets circle rates, runs housing schemes like PMAY for lower-income families, and builds the infrastructure that creates land value.

Regulators

State RERA authorities for projects and agents; SEBI for REITs and InvITs; courts and consumer forums for disputes.

Middlemen

Brokers (who must register with RERA for projects), lawyers, valuers, architects, home inspectors and online property portals.

Investors

Families buying second homes, NRIs like Salim from the forex story, private equity funds, pension funds, and REIT unitholders like Rohit.

Chapter 15

Taxes and selling

Property has its own tax rules, and they've changed often, so Ananya plans to check the current rules with a tax professional when it matters. The broad picture:

  • While you own it: property tax to the town every year. If rented out, rent is taxed as income, after a standard deduction for repairs.
  • Home loan: under the old tax regime, interest on a home loan for a self-occupied home can be deducted up to a limit, and principal repayments count toward the Section 80C limit. The new regime, now the default, doesn't give these deductions for a self-occupied home.
  • When you sell: a gain after holding for more than two years is a long-term capital gain. The rate and the use of inflation indexation changed in July 2024. Reinvesting the gain in another home can, within limits, reduce or remove the tax.
  • TDS: buyers of property above a threshold must deduct tax at source from the payment to the seller.

Selling also takes time and money: brokerage (often 1–2%), clearing the loan, getting the society's no-objection certificate, and waiting for the buyer's loan approval. Meera's advice: "Buy a property you'd be happy to keep, not one you're counting on selling."

Chapter 16

The housewarming

Twenty-six months after booking, Deshmukh's tower receives its occupancy certificate, two months late. Under RERA, Ananya gets a small interest payment for the delay. Her lawyer confirms the sale deed is registered in her name, and Iyer's bank holds the original as security for the loan.

At the housewarming, Meera, now in her nineties, looks out of the eighth-floor window at the bridge she once lent money to build. "That bridge made this land valuable," she says. "My bond, your flat. It's all one story."

Rohit owns a few REIT units. Kamala still owns her plot. Ananya keeps her SIP running and puts any spare money into prepaying her loan.

That is what real estate is: land and the buildings on it, valuable mostly because of where it is. And that is what the real estate market is: the builders, buyers, banks, lawyers, governments and trusts that create, finance, register and trade it, more slowly, expensively and personally than any other market in these stories.

After the story

Eight myths about real estate

Property prices never fall.They have fallen sharply in Japan, the US and China, and stayed flat for years in many Indian cities.
Rent is money wasted.Interest, stamp duty and maintenance are also "wasted". Compare total costs, not rent vs. EMI.
Super built-up area is what you get.You live in the carpet area, often 25–40% smaller.
Under-construction is always cheaper.Add GST, rent while you wait, and the risk of delay.
Land is the safest investment.Unapproved, disputed or badly located land can be impossible to sell.
Paying part in cash saves money.It's illegal, unprovable, unfinanceable, and raises tax when you sell.
A bigger loan is fine if the bank approves it.The bank checks whether you can pay; only you can check whether you can live with it.
REITs are like fixed deposits.Their prices move with markets and payouts can change.

Before buying a property: a checklist

  • The project is registered on the state RERA website, with approvals listed.
  • I've checked the builder's past projects and delivery record.
  • A lawyer has checked the title for 30 years and an encumbrance certificate.
  • I'm comparing price per square foot of carpet area.
  • I've added GST, stamp duty, registration, fees, interiors and deposits.
  • My EMI is comfortably below 40% of take-home pay, with savings left over.
  • I've kept an emergency fund after the down payment.
  • I'll pay under-construction installments only as construction progresses.
  • The full price is paid through banks and recorded in the sale deed.
  • I'd be happy to keep this property for many years.
