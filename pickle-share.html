Part one

A company is born

Prologue

Mango pickle in a steel drum

Lakshmi lives in Kesarpur, three streets from the bridge. Her mango pickle is famous: neighbours, then relatives in other cities, then strangers who heard about it, kept asking for jars. In the first year she sold ₹2 lakh worth from her kitchen, mixed in a steel drum.

By the fifth year the kitchen was a rented shed, there were twelve women working with her, and the shops of three districts stocked her jars under the name Sunrise. Sales were ₹3 crore a year. Big supermarket chains wanted to stock Sunrise across the state. To supply them, Lakshmi would need a real factory, delivery vans, and a year's worth of mangoes bought in season. That would cost about ₹50 lakh, and later much more.

She went to the bank. The bank would lend some money, but wanted her house as security and repayment starting in six months, before the factory would even be running. She thought about the Kesarpur council and its bridge bonds, but bonds need a long track record and steady income to cover fixed interest. A young, fast-growing business doesn't have that.

What Lakshmi needed was someone willing to share the risk: money that didn't have to be repaid on a fixed date, from someone who would be paid only if Sunrise did well. That kind of money is called equity, and the pieces it comes in are called shares.

Third storyThis continues The Kesarpur Bridge Bond and The Kesarpur Common Pot. In the bond story, the town borrowed. Here, a company sells ownership. Kamala, who bought cement shares in the bond story, appears again.
Debt vs. equityA lender (bond, loan) is promised fixed payments and gets nothing extra if the business booms. An owner (share) is promised nothing, but gets a cut of everything the business ever earns.
Chapter 1

What a share is

First, Lakshmi's accountant tells her to turn Sunrise from a home business into a company: Sunrise Foods Private Limited. This sounds like paperwork, but it changes three important things.

  • The company becomes its own legal person. It can own the factory, sign contracts, borrow money and be sued, separately from Lakshmi.
  • Ownership is divided into shares. The company starts with 10,00,000 shares. Lakshmi and her husband own all of them. Whoever owns shares owns that fraction of the company: its factory, its brand, its recipes and its future profits.
  • Liability is limited. If the company fails and owes money, its lenders can take the company's assets, but not Lakshmi's house. Shareholders can lose what they put in, but no more.

Owning a share gives you three kinds of rights:

  • A share of profits. When the company decides to pay out part of its profit, each share gets an equal part. This payout is a dividend.
  • A vote. Shareholders elect the board of directors and vote on big decisions like mergers. One share, one vote. Owning more than half the shares means control.
  • A claim on what is left. If the company is ever sold or closed, shareholders get whatever remains after everyone else, lenders, suppliers, employees and taxes, has been paid.

That last point is crucial. Shareholders come last. In bad times, they may get nothing. In good times, there is no limit to what they can get, because everything left after paying the fixed claims belongs to them. That is why shares are riskier than bonds, and why, over long periods, they have usually paid more.

Share = stock = equityThe words are used almost interchangeably. A share is one unit of ownership; stock often means shares in general; equity means ownership capital.
Face valueShares have a nominal face value, often ₹1, ₹2, ₹5 or ₹10. Unlike a bond's face value, it says almost nothing about what the share is worth. It's an accounting number.
Private vs. publicA private limited company's shares can't be offered to the public and are hard to sell. A public limited company listed on a stock exchange has shares anyone can buy or sell.
Chapter 2

From kitchen to company

Mr. Bose, a retired businessman from Kolkata, tasted Sunrise pickle at a wedding and asked to meet Lakshmi. He is an angel investor: someone who puts personal money into very young companies.

They argue about one number: what is Sunrise worth today? Lakshmi says ₹3 crore. Mr. Bose says ₹1.5 crore, because most young food companies fail. They settle on ₹2 crore. That's the pre-money valuation, the company's value before his money arrives.

Mr. Bose invests ₹50 lakh. The company is now worth ₹2 crore + ₹50 lakh = ₹2.5 crore (the post-money valuation). His ₹50 lakh is 20% of that. To give him 20%, the company issues 2,50,000 new shares at ₹20 each. Lakshmi still has her 10,00,000 shares, but they are now 80% of a larger total.

Three years later, with the factory running and sales at ₹30 crore, a venture capital (VC) fund invests ₹10 crore at a ₹40 crore pre-money valuation. The same arithmetic applies again: 3,12,500 new shares at ₹320 each, for 20% of the company.

Lakshmi's slice shrank from 100% to 64%. This is dilution. But the company grew much faster. Her 64% of ₹50 crore is worth ₹32 crore, far more than her 100% of a kitchen business was worth. And Mr. Bose's 16% stake, bought for ₹50 lakh, is now worth ₹8 crore on paper.

On paper is the key phrase. Neither of them can easily sell. There is no market for shares in a private pickle company. Mr. Bose's money is stuck until something lets him sell: a bigger company buying Sunrise, or Sunrise listing on the stock exchange. That second path is the next chapter.

ValuationWhat a company is worth as a whole. Value per share × number of shares = valuation. Early on it is mostly a negotiation about the future.
DilutionNew shares make each existing share a smaller fraction of the company. It's fine if the new money grows the company enough; harmful if it doesn't.
Why VCs take the riskMost of a VC fund's companies fail or barely return the money. They need one or two huge winners to pay for all the losses. That's why they want big stakes in fast-growing firms.
Chapter 3

Going public

Seven years after the angel round, Sunrise sells in five states. Its profit is ₹30 crore a year. It wants ₹150 crore for three new factories. The VC fund, which promised its own investors their money back within ten years, wants to sell part of its stake. Both needs point the same way: an initial public offering (IPO), the first time a company sells shares to the general public, followed by listing on a stock exchange.

The process takes about a year:

  • Becoming a public company. Sunrise Foods Private Limited becomes Sunrise Foods Limited. It appoints independent directors, sets up an audit committee and tightens its accounting.
  • A share split. Each share is split into 32, so the 15,62,500 shares become 5 crore shares, each worth about ₹10 at the VC's price. Nothing changes in value; there are just more, smaller slices, which suit small investors.
  • The draft offer document. Merchant bankers write a Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP), hundreds of pages long: the business, its risks, its accounts, its legal cases, how the money will be used. It is filed with SEBI and published for comments.
  • Roadshows. Lakshmi and her finance head meet big fund managers in Mumbai, explaining the business and answering hard questions.
  • The price band. The bankers suggest ₹114 to ₹120 per share. At ₹120, the existing 5 crore shares value the company at ₹600 crore, or 20 times its yearly profit.

The IPO has two parts. In the fresh issue, the company creates 1.25 crore new shares and sells them, raising ₹150 crore for the factories. In the offer for sale, the VC sells 50 lakh of its existing shares for ₹60 crore. That money goes to the VC, not the company.

Kamala, now 70 and still fond of shares, applies for one lot: 120 shares at ₹120, which is ₹14,400. Her bank only blocks the money in her account; it is debited only if she gets shares. With 18 retail applications for every lot available, she is lucky. Her friend is not, and her blocked money is released.

Three working days after the IPO closes, Sunrise lists on the NSE and BSE. At 10 am on listing day, after a special pre-open auction, its first price is ₹150, 25% above the issue price. By the close it is ₹141. Kamala's 120 shares are worth ₹16,920.

Lakshmi rings the ceremonial bell at the exchange. Her family now owns 51.2% of Sunrise, worth about ₹450 crore at the listing-day close. The public owns 28%. And thousands of strangers, like Kamala, now own a piece of a pickle company from Kesarpur.

Primary marketThe IPO is the primary market: the company sells new shares and gets the money. After listing, shares trade on the secondary market, and the company receives nothing from those trades.
ASBA"Application supported by blocked amount": your money stays in your account but is blocked until allotment. Most retail IPO applications are now made through UPI.
Grey marketUnofficial trading in IPO shares before listing, quoted as a "grey market premium". It isn't regulated and is often wrong. Treat it as rumour.
Listing gainsSome IPOs list far above their price; many fall below it later. An IPO is often priced when the company and sellers think the timing is best for them.
Part two

The market at work

Chapter 4

Inside the exchange

A month after listing, Kamala's grandson Rohit, a 24-year-old software engineer in Bengaluru, decides to buy Sunrise shares. To do it he needs three accounts: a bank account for money, a trading account with a stockbroker to place orders, and a demat account to hold shares electronically, like a bank account for shares. Most brokers open the last two together, online, after KYC.

He opens his broker's app at 11 am on a Tuesday. The market is open from 9:15 am to 3:30 pm on weekdays. He sees the order book: everyone who currently wants to buy (bids) and sell (asks) Sunrise, and at what prices.

A market order says "buy now at whatever price the sellers are asking". It is filled against the cheapest asks first, then the next cheapest, and so on. A small order barely moves the price. A big one eats through several levels of the book and pushes the last-traded price up. Try moving the slider: this is how buying pressure literally raises a price.

A limit order says "buy, but only at ₹141.00 or less". If no seller is asking that little, the order joins the bids and waits. Rohit uses a limit order, because he doesn't want surprises.

When a buyer and seller match, the trade happens in a fraction of a second, but the money and shares take a little longer to move. The exchange's clearing corporation steps in between them, guaranteeing that the buyer gets shares and the seller gets money even if one side defaults. On the next working day (T+1), the shares arrive in Rohit's demat account, held at a depository (NSDL or CDSL), and the money reaches the seller.

The exchangesIndia's main stock exchanges are the BSE, founded in 1875 and Asia's oldest, and the NSE, which began trading in 1994 and brought fully electronic, screen-based trading.
Bid–ask spreadThe gap between the best bid and the best ask (here 10 paise). Busy, popular shares have tiny spreads; rarely traded shares have wide ones, which is a hidden cost.
T+1Trade day plus one working day. India moved all shares to T+1 settlement by January 2023, one of the fastest in the world.
Costs of a tradeBrokerage, securities transaction tax (STT), exchange charges, GST and stamp duty. Small per trade, but they add up for frequent traders.
Chapter 5

Why the price moves

Nobody sets the price of a Sunrise share. Not Lakshmi, not the exchange, not SEBI. The price is simply the last price at which a willing buyer and a willing seller agreed. When more people want to buy than sell at the current price, buyers have to offer more and the price rises. When more want to sell, the price falls.

But why do people want to buy or sell? Mostly because they are guessing about the future. A share is a claim on all of the company's future profits, so its price reflects what people expect those profits to be, and how sure they are.

Here is one month in Sunrise's life:

Day 22 surprises Rohit. Profit up 40% and the share falls? This is the most important idea about share prices: prices move on surprises, not on news. Good results that everyone expected are already in the price. What moves it is the gap between what happened and what was expected.

Day 15 shows the other big lesson. Nothing changed at Sunrise, but its price fell because the whole market fell. Some of every share's movement comes from the company itself, and some from the market's mood. In the short run, mood often dominates. In the long run, profits do. The investor Benjamin Graham put it this way: in the short run the market is a voting machine; in the long run, it is a weighing machine.

Market capitalisation is the price times all the shares: at ₹144, Sunrise's market cap is ₹144 × 6.25 crore shares = ₹900 crore. That is what the market, at that moment, thinks the whole company is worth.

Market capShare price × total shares. It is used to group companies: large caps (top 100 in India), mid caps (101–250) and small caps (the rest).
Free floatShares actually available to trade. Lakshmi's family's 51.2% rarely trades, so Sunrise's free float is about half its shares.
VolumeHow many shares traded. A big move on high volume says many people agree; a big move on tiny volume may mean little.

Profit can be faked with accounting; cash is much harder to fake. Healthy companies turn most profit into cash.

Neha's quick health check for Sunrise: sales and profit are growing, margins are steady, debt is low, and operating cash flow (₹55 crore) is higher than profit (₹45 crore), which is a good sign that the profit is real. She also reads the parts most beginners skip: the notes to the accounts, the auditor's report, the list of related-party transactions (deals with the founder's family), and the shareholding pattern, which shows whether the promoters have pledged their shares as security for loans.

One warning sign she always looks for: profit growing fast while cash flow from operations stays flat or falls. That can mean customers aren't actually paying, or that something is being dressed up. In 2009, the founder of Satyam Computer Services confessed that he had inflated the company's cash balance by thousands of crores over years, one of India's biggest accounting frauds.

Chapter 7

What is a share worth?

Sunrise shares trade at ₹180. Is that cheap or expensive? Neha teaches Rohit the basic yardsticks, using the statements from Chapter 6. Sunrise has 6.25 crore shares.

A P/E of 25 means buyers are paying ₹25 for every ₹1 of this year's profit. Put the other way round, the earnings yield is 1 ÷ 25 = 4%, less than a government bond pays. Why would anyone accept that? Because they expect profit to grow. If Sunrise's profit grows 20% a year, it doubles in about four years, and today's price would be only about 12 times that future profit.

So the price depends on two things: the earnings, and how much the market is willing to pay for them, the P/E. The P/E is where mood lives. When investors are hopeful, they pay 40 times earnings; when frightened, 12. Try it below.

The more rigorous method is a discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation: estimate all the cash the company will produce in future years and discount it back to today, exactly as the bond story discounted coupons. The difference is that a bond's cash flows are promised, while a company's are guesses. Small changes in the guesses, like growth of 15% instead of 20%, change the answer hugely. That's why two careful analysts can value the same share at ₹120 and ₹250.

A useful habit is a margin of safety: buy only when the price is comfortably below your estimate of value, so that if you're somewhat wrong, you don't lose much.

Compare like with likeA P/E only means something against the company's own history, its competitors, and its growth. A fast-growing consumer brand naturally trades at a higher P/E than a slow-growing steel maker.
PEG ratioP/E divided by expected earnings growth (in %). Sunrise: 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25. A rough check of whether growth justifies the price.
Value trapA low P/E isn't automatically cheap. Sometimes the market is correctly expecting profits to fall.
Chapter 8

Dividends, bonuses, splits

Shareholders make money in two ways. Capital gains: selling for more than you paid. And dividends: a share of profits paid out in cash. Sunrise's board decides to pay ₹1.80 a share this year, a quarter of its profit, and keep the rest to build factories. Kamala's 120 shares earn her ₹216.

A company that keeps profits and reinvests them well grows faster, so its share price should rise instead. Young, fast-growing companies often pay little or no dividend; mature companies with fewer growth opportunities pay out more.

Over the years, Sunrise does several other things to its shares. These are called corporate actions.

Kamala is excited about the bonus. "Free shares!" Rohit explains: cutting a pizza into sixteen slices instead of eight doesn't give you more pizza. Bonuses and splits make shares look cheaper and easier to trade, which can help a little with demand, but they don't create value. Only profits do.

Total returnPrice change + dividends. Over long periods, reinvested dividends make up a meaningful part of share market returns. Indices like the Nifty 50 also have a "total return" version that includes them.
Ex-dateThe first day a buyer no longer gets the declared dividend or bonus. The price usually adjusts down on that day.
Chapter 9

Sensex, Nifty and indices

On the evening news, Kamala hears: "The Sensex rose 400 points today." Sunrise isn't in the Sensex. So what is it?

An index is a scoreboard for a group of shares. It combines their prices into a single number, so you can see how the group did. The two most famous in India are:

  • Sensex: 30 large, actively traded companies on the BSE. Launched in 1986, with its base set at 100 for 1978–79.
  • Nifty 50: 50 large companies on the NSE, launched in 1996 with a base of 1,000.

Both are weighted by free-float market cap: bigger companies count for more, and only shares actually available to trade are counted. A 5% jump in the largest bank moves the index far more than a 5% jump in a smaller member.

Indices matter for more than the news. They are the benchmark that fund managers are judged against, and index funds and ETFs simply buy every company in them. When a company is added to the Nifty 50, index funds must buy it, which often lifts its price for a while. Beyond the headline indices there are hundreds of others: mid-cap and small-cap indices, sector indices for banks, IT or pharma, and broad ones like the Nifty 500 that cover most of the market.

Points vs. percent"The Sensex rose 400 points" sounds huge but means little alone. At a level of 80,000, 400 points is 0.5%. Always think in percentages.
RebalancingIndex committees review membership regularly, replacing companies that have shrunk with ones that have grown. The index itself evolves; the Sensex of today holds very different companies from the Sensex of 1986.
Chapter 10

Who is on the other side

Every time Rohit buys a share, someone sells it to him. Every time he sells, someone buys. It's worth asking who those people are, and why they think the opposite trade is a good idea.

Promoters

Founders and their families, like Lakshmi. They usually hold large, steady stakes. Their buying, selling, or pledging of shares is closely watched because they know the company best. Their holdings are disclosed every quarter.

Domestic institutions (DIIs)

Indian mutual funds, insurance companies like LIC, pension funds and banks. Fed by monthly SIPs and premiums, they bring steady money into the market and have become a counterweight when foreign investors sell.

Foreign investors (FPIs)

Global funds investing in Indian shares. Their money can move in and out quickly with global interest rates, the dollar and risk appetite, which can swing the whole market and the rupee.

Retail investors

Individuals like Rohit and Kamala. Their numbers in India have grown enormously since 2020, helped by easy online accounts. Many invest for years; many others trade for days.

Traders and speculators

People and firms trying to profit from short-term price moves: day traders, futures and options traders. They add liquidity, but most individual traders lose money (Chapter 12).

Algorithms and market makers

Computer programs that trade automatically, some in microseconds. Market makers stand ready to buy and sell, earning the spread. A large share of trades in busy shares now involve algorithms.

When Rohit buys Sunrise because he believes it will grow for ten years, the seller might be a foreign fund that needs cash because its own investors are withdrawing, a trader who bought last week and wants a quick profit, or a fund manager who thinks Sunrise is over-priced. One of them will turn out to be right. The market is where all those different views and needs meet and are turned into a single price.

Chapter 11

Bulls, bears and bubbles

Here is Sunrise's share price over its first twenty years as a listed company. Like almost every share, it did not go up in a straight line.

A bull market is a long period of rising prices, when optimism feeds on itself. A bear market is a fall of 20% or more from a peak, when fear feeds on itself. A correction is a smaller fall, of 10–20%.

In Year 6, a frenzy for anything to do with "packaged foods" swept the market. New food companies with no profits listed at 80 times sales. Sunrise's P/E rose to 70. People who had never invested opened accounts because their neighbours were making money. That's a bubble: prices far beyond any reasonable estimate of value, driven by the expectation that someone else will pay even more. It burst in Year 7. Sunrise fell 52%, though its profits kept growing. Many newer food companies fell 90% or disappeared.

In Year 12, a global financial crisis hit. Everything fell, good and bad companies alike. Sunrise fell 45% in five months. Three years later it had recovered all of it.

Indian investors have lived through real versions of this many times: the 1992 crash after the Harshad Mehta securities scam; the dot-com bubble of 2000; 2008, when the Sensex fell more than 60% from its January peak to October; and March 2020, when Covid fears drove it down nearly 40% in about two months, before it recovered to new highs within the year.

Circuit breakersTo slow panic, Indian exchanges halt all trading if the Sensex or Nifty falls 10%, 15% or 20% in a day. Individual shares also have daily price bands (such as 5%, 10% or 20%), beyond which they can't trade that day.
VolatilityHow much a price swings. Small companies are usually more volatile than large ones; single shares more than indices.
RecoveryBroad markets have historically recovered from every crash, sometimes in months, sometimes in years. Individual companies sometimes never do.
Chapter 12

Kamala's expensive lessons

Kamala has owned shares for forty years. She has made money, and she has made every mistake in the book. She tells Rohit about them, one by one, so he doesn't have to repeat them.

The hot tipA WhatsApp group promised a small company would "go 5×". She bought. So did thousands of others, while the people who started the rumour quietly sold to them. The share fell 80%. This is a pump-and-dump scheme, and it is illegal, but the money rarely comes back.
Borrowed moneyIn the Year 6 bubble she bought on margin, using borrowed money. When prices fell, her broker demanded more money (a margin call). She couldn't pay, so her shares were sold at the bottom. Borrowing magnifies losses as well as gains.
Futures and optionsShe tried trading options for quick profits. A few big wins, then steady losses. SEBI's own study of individual F&O traders in India found that about 93% lost money over three years to March 2024.
Everything in one shareShe once had most of her savings in one cement company. When it ran into trouble, her savings halved. Owning many companies, or an index fund, protects against one failure.
Selling winners, keeping losersShe sold Sunrise at ₹200 for a quick profit and kept a falling share "until it comes back". It never did. Investors hate admitting a loss, so they hold losers too long and sell winners too early.
Following the crowdShe bought when everyone was excited and prices were high, and sold when everyone was afraid and prices were low, which is exactly backwards.
Checking every dayWatching prices daily made every dip feel like a disaster and pushed her into needless trades, each with costs and taxes.
Kamala"The companies were never the problem, beta. I was the problem. The market rewarded me when I sat still and punished me when I got clever."
Investing vs. tradingAn investor buys part of a business to hold for years, expecting it to grow. A trader buys a price, hoping to sell it to someone else soon for more. Both are legal; the odds are very different.
DerivativesFutures and options are contracts based on a share or index price. They let you take large positions with little money, which means large losses with little money too.
Chapter 13

Guarding a fair market

A stock market only works if people trust that the game isn't rigged. If insiders could trade on secrets, or companies could lie in their accounts, ordinary investors would stay away and companies like Sunrise couldn't raise money. In India, the guardian is SEBI, the Securities and Exchange Board of India, along with the exchanges themselves.

Here is one case from Sunrise's history. A week before Sunrise announced that it was being bought by a large food company, a manager in its finance team told his brother-in-law. The brother-in-law bought ₹40 lakh of Sunrise shares. When the deal was announced, the shares jumped 30%.

The exchange's surveillance software flagged the unusual buying just before the news. SEBI investigated, traced the phone calls and family link, and found insider trading: trading on important information that the public doesn't have. The brother-in-law had to give up his profits plus a heavy penalty, and both were barred from the market.

The rules that protect Rohit include:

  • Disclosure. Listed companies must publish quarterly results, annual reports and any price-sensitive news promptly to everyone at once, through the exchange.
  • Insider rules. Directors and employees with access to secrets can't trade during "closed windows" around results, and must report their trades.
  • No manipulation. Spreading false rumours, circular trading to fake volume, or front-running clients' orders are all offences.
  • Surveillance. Exchanges put suspicious shares under extra monitoring, demand higher margins, or restrict trading.
  • Corporate governance. Independent directors, audit committees and limits on deals with related parties.
  • Investor protection. Your shares sit in your own demat account, not your broker's. If a broker fails, exchanges have investor protection funds, and SEBI's SCORES portal handles complaints.
Price-sensitive informationResults, mergers, big contracts, management changes, fund-raising. Anything that would likely move the share price must be announced publicly before anyone can trade on it.
Unregistered advisersOnly SEBI-registered investment advisers and research analysts may give paid stock advice. Social-media "finfluencers" selling tips without registration are a common source of losses.
Part three

The market and you

Chapter 14

The market and the economy

Is the stock market just a casino where people bet on prices? No, though parts of it can feel like one. Its real job is in the story you just read: it moved money from savers like Kamala into a company that used it to build factories, hire 2,000 people, and buy mangoes from thousands of farmers.

Raising capital

IPOs and later share issues let growing companies raise money without unaffordable loans. Without a market where those shares can later be sold, angels and VCs like Mr. Bose would be far less willing to fund young companies.

Sharing ownership

Ordinary people can own pieces of the country's most successful businesses and share in their growth, instead of only the founders and the rich.

Pricing and signalling

Share prices are a constant vote on which companies are using money well. A high valuation makes it cheap for a good company to raise more; a falling price is pressure on bad managers.

Wealth effect

When markets rise, people who own shares feel richer and tend to spend more; when markets crash, they cut back. The market feeds back into the economy.

Interest rates

From the bond story: when interest rates rise, safe bonds pay more and future company profits are worth less today, so share prices often fall. Falling rates tend to help shares.

Market ≠ economy

The market looks ahead, so it often rises before the economy recovers and falls before it weakens. It is also dominated by large listed companies, while most jobs are in small, unlisted ones. The two can move apart for long stretches.

Chapter 15

How Rohit invests

Combining Neha's analysis, Kamala's lessons, and his cousin Ananya's mutual fund experience (from The Common Pot), Rohit settles on a plan.

The core: an index fund. Most of his monthly investment goes into a Nifty 50 index fund through a SIP. It's cheap, diversified, and needs no stock picking. He knows from Chapter 6 of the fund story that most active funds trail their index over long periods.

The satellite: a few shares he understands. About 15% of his investments go into five or six companies whose businesses he can explain in two sentences and whose accounts he has read, including Sunrise. No single company is more than 5% of his total. He reviews them each quarter when results come out, not each day.

The rules he writes for himself:

  • Only money he won't need for at least five years goes into shares. Emergency money stays in a bank and a liquid fund.
  • No borrowed money, no tips, no F&O.
  • Before buying any share, write down why, and what would make him sell. Sell only if that reason changes, not because the price did.
  • Keep investing through crashes.
  • Rebalance once a year if shares have grown far beyond his planned mix.

Tax on shares in India

Tax rules change often, so treat this as how the system worked under the rules announced in July 2024. Listed shares held 12 months or less: short-term gains taxed at 20%. Held longer: long-term gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year taxed at 12.5%. Dividends are added to income and taxed at your slab rate. Securities transaction tax (STT) is charged on every trade. Check the latest rules before you sell.

Core and satelliteA low-cost diversified core for most of the money, with a small satellite of individual picks. The core protects against your own mistakes; the satellite lets you learn.
StylesValue investors buy cheap shares; growth investors pay up for fast growers; quality investors want strong, steady businesses; index investors buy everything. Each has had long good and bad periods.
Time in the marketMissing just a handful of the best days in a decade can sharply cut long-run returns, and those days often come right after the worst ones. Staying invested beats trying to jump in and out.
Chapter 16

Twenty years of pickle

Twenty years after listing, Sunrise Foods sells pickles, chutneys and spice mixes in twenty countries. Lakshmi, now in her seventies, is chairperson. Her daughter runs the company. Two thousand people work in its factories, and its mango orders keep thousands of farming families in business.

Kamala's 120 IPO shares, after a 1:1 bonus and a 1:5 split, are now 1,200 shares trading at about ₹184 each, worth about ₹2.2 lakh, plus years of dividends, from ₹14,400 invested.

After the split, Sunrise's market cap is far larger than the ₹900 crore of its early years, because the company itself has grown many times over. She sold half of them twice in panics and bought back higher both times. Had she simply held, she would have had more. She laughs about it now.

Rohit, at 44, has a portfolio mostly in index funds, with a few shares he still follows each quarter. He has lived through two crashes without selling.

That is what a share is: a small piece of a real business, entitled to a slice of everything it will ever earn, and last in line if things go wrong. And that is what the stock market is: the place where millions of people with different hopes, fears and time horizons buy and sell those pieces every second, and in doing so decide what every company is worth.

The three storiesThe bond lent money for a fixed return. The mutual fund pooled savings so ordinary people could own many investments. The share made them owners. Together they are how savings become bridges, factories and jobs.

Eight myths about the stock market

It's gambling.Trading on tips and derivatives can be. Owning a spread of profitable businesses for years is ownership, and has historically been rewarded.
A ₹50 share is cheaper than a ₹5,000 share.Price per share says nothing. Compare P/E, growth and quality. A ₹50 share can be far more expensive.
You need a lot of money to start.You can buy a single share, or start an index fund SIP with a few hundred rupees.
It has fallen so much, it must go back up.Prices have no memory. A share that fell 80% can fall another 80%.
Good company = good investment.Only at the right price. A great company bought at a bubble price can lose money for a decade.
IPOs always give quick profits.Many list below their issue price or fall after the first day. Read the prospectus, not the excitement.
Experts can predict the market.No one reliably predicts short-term moves. Plans that need accurate predictions usually fail.
Bonus shares make you richer.They cut the same pie into more slices. Only profit growth creates value.

Before you buy a share: a checklist

  • I can explain what the company does and how it makes money.
  • I've read its last annual report, including the notes and the auditor's report.
  • Sales, profit and operating cash flow have grown together over several years.
  • Debt is manageable, and promoters haven't pledged much of their stake.
  • I've compared its P/E with its history, its peers and its growth.
  • I know why I'm buying, and what would make me sell.
  • It's money I won't need for at least five years.
  • No single share is too large a part of my savings.
  • I'm not acting on a tip, a rumour, or fear of missing out.
  • I'm not using borrowed money.

Glossary

Bear / bull market
A fall of 20%+ from a peak / a long rise.
Bid / ask
Highest price a buyer offers / lowest price a seller accepts.
Blue chip
A large, well-established, financially strong company.
Bonus issue
Free extra shares to existing holders; value unchanged.
Book value
Shareholders' equity per share in the accounts.
Circuit breaker
An automatic trading halt after a large fall.
Demat account
Electronic account that holds your shares.
Dilution
Existing holders' share shrinking as new shares are issued.
Dividend
Part of profit paid to shareholders in cash.
EPS
Earnings per share: net profit ÷ shares.
Ex-date / record date
Cut-off dates for receiving dividends or bonuses.
F&O
Futures and options: derivative contracts on shares or indices.
Free float
Shares available for public trading.
Index
A number tracking a group of shares, like the Nifty 50.
Insider trading
Trading on important non-public information; illegal.
IPO
Initial public offering: first sale of shares to the public.
Limit / market order
Trade only at a set price or better / trade now at the best available price.
Margin
Borrowing from a broker to buy shares.
Market cap
Share price × total shares.
P/E ratio
Price ÷ earnings per share.
Promoter
The founder or controlling group of a company.
Rights issue
Offer of new shares to existing holders, usually at a discount.
ROE
Return on equity: profit ÷ shareholders' equity.
Share split
Dividing each share into several; value unchanged.
Stock exchange
A regulated marketplace for shares, like the NSE and BSE.
T+1
Settlement one working day after the trade.
Volatility
How much a price swings up and down.
