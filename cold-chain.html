A story about private markets, in sixteen chapters

The Kesarpur Cold Chain

Every summer, Kesarpur's farmers watch tomatoes and mangoes rot for lack of cold storage. Meghna, Lakshmi's daughter, has a plan to fix it with solar-powered cold rooms, but no bank will lend to an idea. Follow her company from a laptop to a ₹300 crore valuation, and see the hidden world of money that never touches a stock exchange: angels, venture capital, private equity and private credit.

Part one

Funding a startup

Prologue

Rotting tomatoes

Meghna grew up watching her mother Lakshmi build Sunrise Foods from a kitchen into a listed company (the stock market story). She studied engineering, worked three years at a logistics company, and came home with an idea.

In Kesarpur, Gopal's farmer group loses up to a third of its tomatoes and soft fruit in hot months because there's nowhere cold to keep them before sale. Meghna wants to put small solar-powered cold rooms in villages, rent space by the crate through an app, and connect stored produce to buyers in cities.

She needs about ₹2 crore to build the first ten cold rooms and the software. A bank asks for collateral and three years of profits. She has neither. A startup with an unproven idea can't borrow; it needs investors willing to take the risk of losing everything for the chance of a big win.

Those investors don't buy shares on a stock exchange. They invest privately, in companies most people have never heard of. This is the world of private markets.

Eleventh storyThis follows the ten earlier stories. Mr. Bose (the angel from the stock market story), Gopal, Harjeet, Lakshmi and Kamala return.
Who this is forMost private market investments are open only to wealthy and institutional investors. This story explains how they work, not how to get into them.
Chapter 1

What private markets are

Public markets are the markets in most of the earlier stories: shares and bonds listed on exchanges, priced every second, open to anyone with a demat account. Private markets are investments in companies and assets that aren't listed. They're bought and sold through private negotiations, priced rarely, and usually held for years.

IlliquidityBeing unable to sell quickly. Investors in private markets expect extra return for accepting it, called the illiquidity premium. They don't always get it.
Chapter 2

The angel returns

Meghna's first call is to Mr. Bose, the retired businessman who backed her mother's pickle company decades ago and made over 20 times his money when Sunrise listed. He now leads a group of angel investors in Kolkata.

Mr. Bose"I'm not investing in cold rooms. I'm investing in you, and in a problem big enough that if you solve it, the company could be worth a hundred times what it is today. Most of my startups fail. I need the few winners to be huge."

After meeting her three times, visiting a prototype cold room in Gopal's village and calling her old boss, Mr. Bose and four other angels invest ₹1.5 crore. They agree the company is worth ₹6 crore before the money arrives (the pre-money valuation, as in the stock story), so their ₹1.5 crore buys 20% of the ₹7.5 crore post-money company.

In India, early investors usually receive compulsorily convertible preference shares (CCPS): shares that come with extra rights now and convert into ordinary shares later. Some very early rounds use convertible notes, which turn into shares at the next funding round's price.

Angel investorA wealthy individual investing personal money in early startups, often adding advice and contacts.
Angel tax abolishedIndia used to tax some startup investments above "fair value" as income, which deterred angels. The 2024 Union Budget abolished this "angel tax".
Expect lossesExperienced angels typically expect most of their startups to fail or return little. They spread small cheques across many companies.
Chapter 3

The cap table

Every startup keeps a capitalisation table, or cap table: a record of who owns what. Each new funding round issues new shares, so everyone else's percentage shrinks. That's dilution, from the stock market story. Here's Meghna's company, Kesar Cold Chain, over four years.

ESOP poolShares set aside for employees through stock options. Startups pay lower salaries and offer a share of the upside to attract people.
Paper wealthUntil there's an exit (Chapter 11), these valuations are estimates. Nobody can easily sell at ₹300 crore.
Chapter 4

Inside a venture fund

For Series A, Meghna meets Tara, a partner at a venture capital fund that invests in agriculture and supply-chain startups. Tara doesn't invest her own money. She manages money from others.

Limited partners (LPs)

The investors in the fund: pension funds, insurers (like those in the insurance story), university endowments, family offices, wealthy individuals, and government funds of funds. In India, SIDBI's Fund of Funds for Startups, launched in 2016, has invested in many VC funds.

General partner (GP)

Tara's firm. It finds startups, negotiates deals, sits on boards and helps companies grow. It makes all investment decisions.

The deal

LPs commit, say, ₹500 crore for about 10 years. The GP draws it down as needed, typically investing for the first 4–5 years and helping companies grow for the rest. The GP is paid a yearly management fee (often around 2%) and a share of profits (often 20%), called carried interest.

In India, these funds are registered with SEBI as alternative investment funds (AIFs). Category I includes venture capital and angel funds; Category II includes private equity and private debt funds; Category III includes hedge-fund-like strategies that can use leverage.

Chapter 5

One winner pays for all

Tara's fund invests ₹5 crore each in 20 startups. She knows most will disappoint. Venture returns follow a power law: a tiny number of huge winners produce almost all the profit. Change how many big winners the fund finds and watch the whole fund's result.

Invested₹100 cr
Returned₹235 cr
Fund multiple (before fees)2.35×

This is why VCs ask every founder, "Could this be enormous?" A safe business that might return 3× isn't interesting to them; only companies that could return the whole fund are. It's also why VC-backed companies are pushed to grow fast, sometimes too fast.

Power lawA pattern where a few outcomes are vastly larger than all the rest combined. Venture returns, book sales and city sizes follow it.
UnicornA private startup valued at over $1 billion. India passed 100 unicorns in 2022.
Chapter 6

The term sheet

Tara sends Meghna a term sheet: a short document listing the deal's key terms before lawyers write the full agreements. Mr. Bose helps her read it. The valuation is in the first line, but other clauses can matter as much.

  • Valuation and amount: ₹15 crore at ₹45 crore pre-money.
  • Liquidation preference: if the company is sold, investors get their money back first, before founders and employees.
  • Board seat: Tara joins the board.
  • Protective provisions: investor approval needed for big decisions like selling the company or raising more money.
  • Anti-dilution: if a future round is at a lower price, investors' shares are partly adjusted to protect them.
  • Vesting: founders' and employees' shares are earned over time, usually four years, so someone who leaves early doesn't keep everything.

The liquidation preference is the one founders most often misunderstand. Try different sale prices for the company after Series B and see who gets what. Series A and B each have a "1× non-participating" preference: each can take either its money back or its share of the sale, whichever is more.

Headline vs. realityA high valuation with harsh terms (2× or "participating" preferences) can leave founders and employees with little even in a decent sale.
Due diligenceBefore signing, investors check finances, contracts, legal issues and technology. Founders should check their investors too.
Chapter 7

The J-curve

Tara's fund looks terrible for its first few years. A pension fund that committed ₹100 crore keeps sending money as the fund calls it, pays fees, and sees some startups fail early, while the winners take years to grow. Only later, when companies are sold or listed, does money come back. Plotted over time, the investor's cumulative cash looks like the letter J.

Capital callThe fund asking LPs to send part of their commitment. LPs must keep cash ready, sometimes for years.
Vintage yearThe year a fund starts investing. Funds that invest at market peaks often do worse, so investors spread commitments across years.
Chapter 8

Measuring returns, and fees

Private funds report returns differently from mutual funds, and the numbers need careful reading.

  • TVPI (total value to paid-in): everything returned plus the estimated value of what's still held, divided by money invested. Part of it is just an estimate.
  • DPI (distributions to paid-in): only the cash actually returned. "DPI is the only number you can spend," as investors say.
  • IRR: the yearly return accounting for the timing of each cash flow, like XIRR in the fund story. IRR can look flattering if money is returned early, even if the total multiple is modest.

Then there are fees. A fund charging "2 and 20" takes a 2% yearly management fee and 20% of profits as carried interest (usually after investors earn a minimum return, the hurdle). Here's how a strong gross return shrinks for the investor.

Investor gets back₹212 cr
Net multiple2.12×
Paid to the manager₹48 cr

Simplified: ₹100 crore fund, 2% fees for 10 years (₹20 crore), so ₹80 crore is invested in companies; 20% carry on profit above the ₹100 crore committed. Real funds differ in fee schedules and hurdles.

Stale marksUnlisted holdings are valued by the fund itself, often quarterly. In a falling market, they can stay high for a long time, making private funds look smoother than they really are.
Part two

Private equity, credit and exits

Chapter 9

The cement company buyout

Venture capital funds young companies. Private equity usually buys into established ones. Remember the struggling cement company from the bond and stock stories, the one with junk-rated bonds? It's now profitable but badly run. Its founding family wants to retire.

Vikram's private equity fund buys the whole company for ₹1,000 crore, 8 times its yearly operating profit (EBITDA) of ₹125 crore. It pays with ₹400 crore of the fund's money and ₹600 crore borrowed against the company itself. This is a leveraged buyout (LBO).

Over five years, the fund brings in new managers, cuts waste, upgrades a kiln, and uses the company's cash to repay half the debt. Then it sells. How much the fund makes depends on how much profit grows and what the next buyer will pay.

Fund's equity at sale₹1,038 cr
Multiple on ₹400 cr2.6×
Yearly return (IRR)21%

Three engines drive a buyout's return: growing profit, paying down debt, and selling at a higher multiple than you paid. The borrowed money amplifies all three, just like Ananya's home loan in the real estate story. If profits fall, the debt can sink the company. Buyouts have saved many badly run businesses, and broken some with too much debt.

EBITDAEarnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: a rough measure of cash profit from operations, from the stock story.
Growth equityNot every PE deal is a buyout. Many Indian PE funds take minority stakes in growing profitable firms, without heavy debt.
Chapter 10

Private credit

Harjeet, the petrol pump owner, wants to open a highway truck stop with fuel, food, rest rooms and EV charging. He needs ₹12 crore. Iyer's cooperative bank can lend only ₹4 crore; it's too big and unusual for them. Harjeet's business is too small to issue bonds like Kesarpur's council did.

A private credit fund lends him the rest: ₹8 crore for five years at about 13%, higher than a bank's rate. In return, Harjeet gets speed and flexibility. The fund protects itself with covenants, like those in the bridge bond: limits on further borrowing, minimum cash cover, and quarterly accounts.

Private credit has grown fast worldwide as banks have become more cautious. Investors like it for its higher yields. The risks are those of the debt fund in the mutual fund story, without the daily price: if borrowers struggle, losses may surface only when a loan defaults.

Direct lendingA fund lending directly to a company, without a bank or public bond market in between.
Venture debtLoans to VC-backed startups, often with a small equity sweetener. Meghna uses some to fund cold rooms without giving up more shares.
Chapter 11

Getting the money out

Private investors only make real money when they can sell. That's called an exit, and it can take 5–10 years or more. There are four main routes.

  • IPO: listing on the stock exchange, like Sunrise in the stock story. Investors can then sell shares in the market, usually after a lock-in.
  • Sale to a strategic buyer: a larger company buys the startup. In 2018, Walmart bought a 77% stake in Flipkart for about $16 billion, one of India's largest startup exits.
  • Secondary sale: an investor sells its stake to another investor, such as a later-stage fund, without the company listing.
  • Shutdown: the company fails; investors usually get nothing.
Lock-inAfter an IPO, early investors and founders are usually barred from selling for a period, to stop a flood of shares hitting the market.
Chapter 12

When it goes wrong

Private markets have their own ways to fail, often hidden for longer because there's no daily price.

Down roundsRaising money at a lower valuation than the last round. Existing investors and employees are diluted, and anti-dilution clauses shift more value to investors. After the cheap-money boom of 2021, many startups faced down rounds or couldn't raise at all.
Growth at any costFunding pushes startups to grow fast, sometimes by burning cash on discounts and ads. When funding dries up, they shrink or close.
Governance failuresEdtech company Byju's was valued at about $22 billion in 2022. It then faced delayed accounts, auditor and board resignations, lawsuits from lenders and insolvency proceedings, and its value collapsed. Private companies face less public scrutiny than listed ones.
Too much debtBuyouts that borrow too much can fail if profits dip, leaving lenders and employees to absorb losses.
FraudWithout public disclosure, inflated revenues or fake customers can go unnoticed until a funding round's due diligence, or a collapse.
Survivorship againYou hear about the unicorns. Thousands of startups that quietly closed don't make headlines.
Part three

Who can invest, and should they?

Chapter 13

Can ordinary people invest?

Mostly, no. Indian rules deliberately keep private markets for investors who can afford to lose money and wait years.

  • AIFs usually require a minimum investment of ₹1 crore per investor.
  • Portfolio management services (PMS) require ₹50 lakh.
  • Angel funds and accredited investor routes are aimed at people with high income or large net worth, verified under SEBI's accredited investor framework.

Ordinary investors get indirect exposure in other ways: by buying shares of companies after they list, through mutual funds that own those listed companies, or through pension and insurance money that invests in private funds on their behalf.

Kamala's "pre-IPO" shares

Kamala, true to form, gets a WhatsApp message: "Buy unlisted shares of a famous startup before its IPO! Guaranteed 3× on listing." The seller asks for payment to a personal account and promises to "transfer the shares later".

Rohit stops her. Unlisted shares sold through messages, with promises of guaranteed listing gains and payments to individuals, are a common fraud. Even genuine unlisted shares can be hard to verify and sell, with no guarantee the company will ever list, or list at a higher price. SEBI has warned investors about unregulated platforms and offers of unlisted securities.

Red flags"Guaranteed" listing gains, urgency, payments to personal accounts, no demat transfer, sellers you can't verify.
EmployeesStartup employees often hold ESOPs. These can be valuable, but are illiquid and taxed in specific ways. Some companies organise buybacks for employees.
Chapter 14

Private markets at scale

Zoom out from Kesarpur. Globally, private equity, venture, private credit and private infrastructure funds manage many trillions of dollars, and many companies now stay private for much longer than they used to. India has become one of the world's largest startup ecosystems.

Who supplies the money

Global pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurers, endowments, family offices and, increasingly, Indian institutions and wealthy families. Foreign investors have funded most of India's large startup rounds.

Who manages it

Global PE firms, Indian and international VC funds, private credit funds, and angel networks. Many Indian startups have also been backed by large technology companies.

Government role

SEBI regulates AIFs and accredited investors. The Startup India programme, the government's fund of funds through SIDBI, and the 2024 removal of angel tax aim to make startup funding easier.

Cycles

Private markets boom when money is cheap. Startup funding in India hit a record in 2021, then fell sharply as interest rates rose worldwide, the so-called funding winter.

Link to public markets

Many recent Indian IPOs are of VC- and PE-backed companies. Public market investors often buy the shares that private investors are selling.

Concerns

High fees, opaque valuations, conflicts of interest, and the risk that ordinary savers are offered private products they can't fully assess.

Chapter 15

Rohit's view

Rohit asks Tara, over coffee, whether he should try to invest in startups. Her honest answer surprises him.

Tara"Not yet, and maybe never. Even professionals with full-time teams and access to the best deals often fail to beat a simple index fund after fees. If you ever have enough money that you could lose a crore without it mattering, then think about a small slice, through a good fund, spread across years. Until then, own the companies after they list."

His rules, if he ever does:

  • Only money he won't need for 10 years and could lose entirely.
  • A small share of his total wealth, after everything in the earlier stories is in place.
  • Through SEBI-registered funds with long track records, or as part of a reputable angel network that spreads money across many startups.
  • Read net returns and DPI, not headline valuations or gross IRR.
  • Never through WhatsApp offers, "pre-IPO" schemes or unverified sellers.
PatiencePrivate investing rewards those who can wait a decade and lose individual bets without panic. That's rarer than it sounds.
Chapter 16

Forty villages later

Six years after her first cold room, Kesar Cold Chain runs storage in forty villages across three districts. Gopal's group now loses less than 5% of its tomatoes instead of a third, and sells in cities at better prices. Meghna still owns over 40% of the company and is preparing for a listing.

Mr. Bose's angel group invested in twelve startups that year. Seven have closed. Three are alive but small. One was sold for about what they put in. And one, Meghna's, is worth more than all the others combined, many times over. "That," he says, "is the whole game."

Vikram's fund sold the cement company to a larger cement group after five years, for about 2.5 times its money. Harjeet's truck stop opened on time and is repaying its private loan ahead of schedule. Kamala didn't buy the "pre-IPO" shares.

That is what private markets are: investments in companies and assets that don't trade on any exchange, from a startup's first cheque to a billion-rupee buyout. And that is how they work: patient, expensive, high-risk money that builds new companies and remakes old ones, managed by professionals for investors who can afford to wait, and to lose.

After the story

Seven myths about private markets

Private investments are safer because prices don't jump around.They're only priced less often. The risk is there; you just don't see it daily.
A high valuation means the founders are rich.Until an exit, it's on paper, and preferences can put investors first in a sale.
VCs pick winners most of the time.Most of their investments fail; a few winners carry the fund.
Private equity always beats the stock market.After fees and with honest marks, many funds don't.
IRR tells you how much you made.IRR measures speed, not size. Check multiples and cash returned.
"Pre-IPO" shares always rise on listing.Many IPOs list below private prices, and some companies never list.
Every startup will become a unicorn.Most startups close within a few years.

Before any private investment: a checklist

  • My emergency fund, insurance and core investments are already in place.
  • I can lock this money away for 7–12 years.
  • I could lose it all without real harm.
  • It's through a SEBI-registered AIF, PMS or reputable angel network.
  • I understand all fees: management fee, carry, hurdle, expenses.
  • I've seen the manager's past funds' DPI and net returns, not just paper values.
  • I'm spreading commitments across companies and years.
  • I'm not acting on an unsolicited "pre-IPO" or "guaranteed" offer.
